Phone-Ban Study Becomes the Day’s Biggest Story, Up 861 Points Since Morning

The first national before-and-after analysis — 3.3 million students at 4,600 schools — found strict bans lift math and reading as if students had an extra week or two of school a year. At the Morning Edition’s snapshot it stood at 225 points; by 22:00 UTC it had 1,086 and the day’s largest comment count in waiting.

CAMBRIDGE, MASS. — By the Culture Desk★ 1086 pts · ▲ +861 since the last edition

The New York Times reported Tuesday that school cellphone bans are beginning to improve academic performance, citing the first large national analysis to compare test scores before and after bans, from Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research. The study followed 3.3 million students in third through tenth grade at 4,600 U.S. schools from 2021 to 2026, using fall-to-spring MAP test growth to compare strict-ban schools — phones locked in pouches all school day — with similar schools that had no ban. The gains equaled an additional week or two of school per year and reversed about half of the average annual loss students recorded before the pandemic.[1][2]

“The earlier studies on cellphone bans have been dismissed as implying small impacts,” Thomas Kane of the Harvard Graduate School of Education, an author of the study, told the Times; “But we think that’s mistaken.” The Times itself sits behind a bot wall; the article’s findings are as verified in this morning’s reporting and restated in the thread. The story’s rise — 225 points at the Morning Edition snapshot to 1,086 by this one, +861 points, near a fivefold multiple — is the largest single-edition swing in recent editions.[1][2]

The thread’s sharpest argument is about mechanism, not measurement: several readers who went to school before the smartphone argued the harm arrived with around-the-clock social media rather than the phone itself — recess spent sharing MP3s over infrared being, as one put it, “a far cry” from a device that is “one big 24/7 public group.” Others pointed at enforcement: teachers who could not confiscate phones without a parent dispute stopped trying.[2]

OpenAI Ships GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, Cutting Frontier Rates the Day Before Anthropic’s IPO

AI Desk★ 906 pts · 545 cmts

OpenAI introduced GPT-6.2 Astra, its new frontier model, and GPT-6.2 Sol, its everyday model, available immediately to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex, and through the API as gpt-6.2-astra and gpt-6.2-sol. On Agents’ Last Exam, which tests multistep professional work, the company says Astra scores 5.2 points higher than GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, and 5.5 points ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 at 5 percent of the cost.[3][4]

API rates for Astra fall 20 percent on input and output from GPT-6 Astra, with a 60 percent discount on cached input: $8 for input, $0.40 for cached input and $40 for output per million tokens. Sol keeps GPT-6.1 Sol’s rates of $2, $0.10 and $10. On Harvey LAB-AA v1.1, a legal-assignments benchmark, OpenAI says Astra more than doubles Fable 5.2’s pass rate at about a sixth of the cost, with an Astra for Law configuration scoring higher still; on internal GDPval v2 it leads with 6.2 points over GPT-6 Astra while using 22 percent fewer output tokens.[3]

The thread timed the release against Anthropic’s IPO — “it’s actually in OpenAI’s interests that Anthropic’s IPO is successful,” one reader countered — noted the linked system card was not yet up at lunchtime, and split on practical differences: several practitioners said Astra earns its keep coordinating subagents and cleaning up Sol’s output; others called Sol the better default.[4]

NSA’s Classified Internal Network Is Down; the Agency Calls It “Scheduled Routine Maintenance”

Security Desk★ 353 pts · 474 cmts

NSANet, the highly classified network the National Security Agency uses for internal communications, is down, CNN reported Tuesday, citing six people familiar with the matter. There is no evidence of a cyberattack, the sources said; the agency has enacted staggered arrivals for personnel while it works to understand the scope of the problem. The report was published at 10:31 a.m. ET and updated through the afternoon.[7][8]

“The National Security Agency experienced temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance,” the agency said in a statement. “While some internal systems were affected, mission-critical capabilities remain fully operational and our ability to defend national security continues.” White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said on Newsmax there was “no crisis, or anything like that, for Americans to be worried about.” A source told CNN restoration was ongoing but not complete.[7]

Officials said the outage would not affect urgent intelligence reaching President Trump, traveling in Asia on Air Force One — in the Philippines, with talks in China ahead — over backup networks. NSANet is “more of a network of networks, and it’s unlikely that a full outage exists given the many redundancies in place,” a former senior NSA official told CNN, though it “could impact analysis and reporting.”[7]

83 Billion Tokens Later, a Harness Leaderboard Finds the Best Coding-Agent Harness Depends on the Model

Tools Desk★ 297 pts · 105 cmts

Runta, with Baseten, ran 22 harness configurations against five open-weight models on 62 terminal tasks with four attempts each — 27,280 agent runs and 83 billion tokens, 76 times the size of its v1.0 leaderboard. The finding: when you switch models, consider switching harnesses. Each model had a different best-value harness — Codex CLI with GLM-5.3, Pi with GLM-5.3 Flash, ZCode with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, Oh My Pi with Muse, and Alma with Kimi-K3.[9][10]

Cost rankings moved sharply between models: the median harness shifted 11 of 22 places, and Codex CLI went from first with GLM-5.3 to sixteenth with Kimi-K3. Across all models, four harnesses sat on the cost-pass frontier — OpenCode v2 lowest at $0.561 per task, ZCode highest pass rate at 61.9 percent — with the rest costing up to 1.6 times as much. On any single model, cost per pass varies by up to 4.4 times.[9]

Caveats came from the thread itself: the founder, Guanlan, disclosed the eval runs on Runta’s own runtime — “weigh this accordingly” — and one reader noted that explaining cost differences by “steps per pass” is nearly tautological, since run cost is proportional to tokens produced.[10]

Nvidia Stopped Disclosing Singapore Sales as Subsidiary Revenue Hit $104B

Field Desk★ 236 pts · 122 cmts

Nvidia stopped disclosing the scale of its business in Singapore as revenues routed through its Singapore subsidiary hit $104 billion, the Financial Times reports — at one point the city-state accounted for almost half of the company’s worldwide revenue. The article sits behind a bot wall; the figures above are from its headline, and the detail below from the thread.[11][12]

The thread’s most-cited clarification: the $104 billion is the subsidiary’s 2025 external revenue — server-makers route orders through Singapore for delivery elsewhere — not sales to Singapore customers, which Nvidia’s earlier reporting had put at $2 billion to $4 billion by billing location. Nvidia’s July 2025 quarterly filing showed $10 billion in a quarter under “Customer Billing Location”; the disclosure categories changed afterward.[12]

The context is the Megaspeed affair: chips shipped toward Malaysia reportedly ended up in containers of unopened boxes rather than running racks, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the Treasury, Defense and State departments and the USTR had urged Commerce to add Megaspeed to the Entity List. Readers contested how much smuggling was ever verified — renting Singapore compute to Chinese customers was, for a time, legal.[12]

Claude Code Team Says 90 Percent of Its Code Is Written and Reviewed by Claude

AI Desk★ 233 pts · 179 cmts

On the Claude Code team, “the vast majority of our code (~90%) is written and reviewed by Claude,” writes Addy Osmani on claude.dev — which makes verification, not writing, the bottleneck. Verification loops are “the building block that lets you delegate more work to Claude”: without them “you can really only run one or two Claudes at a time, because you have to check everything yourself”; with them, dozens or hundreds in parallel.[13][14]

The team’s evidence: in internal testing on structured file generation, a separate grader checking Claude’s work against a rubric improved task success by up to 10 points over a standard prompting loop, with the largest gains on the hardest problems. The essay walks through three test types worth investing in — end-to-end, property and differential — and closes the loop by turning repeated failures into hooks and explicit rules.[13]

The thread’s top-rated reply is four words of due diligence: “I am more interested in the remaining 10%.” Others pushed on the parallel-scale claim — one reader already spends $2,000 a month on tokens running one or two agents — and on what verification costs when the verifier is also a model.[14]

Microsoft’s MAI Code 2 Claims 98 Percent of GPT 6.1 Sol at 40 Percent of the Cost

AI Desk★ 205 pts · 101 cmts

Microsoft AI announced MAI Code 2 and MAI Code 2 Flash, two coding models aimed at everyday enterprise work: Code 2 for taking code to production, deep debugging, multi-file implementation, repo-wide reasoning and UI; Flash for high-volume work such as explanations, targeted edits and routine fixes. Averaged over four benchmarks — DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal Bench 2.1, SWE-Atlas and FrontierCode v1.1 — Microsoft says Code 2 delivers 98 percent of GPT 6.1 Sol’s performance at 40 percent of the cost, and 96 percent of Claude Opus 5.5’s at 5 percent of the cost.[15][16]

The company’s own framing invites the precision: the models are “explicitly not yet tuned for harder problems, like fundamental science research and very long horizon agentic work,” and the four-benchmark average targets “the everyday enterprise coding tasks we see in GitHub Copilot.” The thread added that the listed rate is a 50 percent promotional discount, with comparisons taken at maximum reasoning effort for the rival models.[15][16]

Readers asked for DeepSeek, GLM and Qwen comparisons, and argued distribution over quality: every large corporation already holds a GitHub subscription, one noted, so MAI’s realistic lane may be Copilot rather than the frontier.[16]

Culture Desk

VisualBasic.World Archives 2,500 Planet Source Code Programs by Their Screenshots

Culture Desk★ 202 pts · 77 cmts

VisualBasic.World is a gallery of Planet Source Code programs from 1997 to 2012, browsed through their original screenshots — “a visual archive of the things we made in Visual Basic.” The collection draws on an archive of more than 15,000 projects, with 2,500 currently shown, weighted toward popular submissions across six categories: Graphics, Games, DirectX, Complete Applications, Custom Controls/Forms/Menus, and Sound/MP3.[17][18]

The thread read it as interface history. The gallery is “nightmare fuel for modern designers who are afraid of the concept of ‘useful information density,’” one reader wrote; a reply allowed that some of it was nightmare fuel for users too, but pressed the point: “somehow our screens are higher resolution than ever but showing less information than we used to show on 14” CRTs running at 800x600.”[18]

The nostalgia came with its own ledger: Visual Basic was “a sloppy language to code in, the runtimes were a pain to manage,” and everything was tied to one proprietary operating system — and yet, the same reader concluded, it was “a little silly” that it remained a better tool for building desktop GUIs as a teenager than what came after.[18]

Corrections & Clarifications

“Gradually,” precisely. The headline says phone bans are “gradually improving test scores”; the measured effect belongs to strict bans — phones locked in pouches for the whole school day — and equals “an additional week or two of school a year,” reversing about half of the average pre-pandemic annual loss. Looser bans are not what the study measured.[1]

The $104B is the subsidiary, not the island. The FT headline pairs “Singapore sales” with “$104B”; the thread’s clarification is that the figure is the 2025 external revenue of Nvidia’s Singapore subsidiary — orders routed through the entity — while sales booked to customers in Singapore were earlier reported at $2B–$4B. Singapore lacks the data-center space and grid to run the chips itself; where they ran is the open question.[11][12]

“98% of GPT 6.1 Sol at 40% of the cost” is an average of four. Microsoft’s figure is averaged over DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal Bench 2.1, SWE-Atlas and FrontierCode v1.1 — benchmarks chosen for “everyday enterprise coding tasks” — and the company states the models are “explicitly not yet tuned for harder problems.” The thread notes the comparison rate is a 50 percent promotional discount taken against rivals at maximum reasoning effort.[15][16]

“Apple Services Down” is set from the thread, not the page. The linked developer status page renders only in a browser, and both of Apple’s status pages listed failing services — sign-ins, Apple Cash, App Store Connect — as operating at snapshot.
