The PHP2Shell audit returns: finding bugs got cheap, fixing them did not

A 2024 crash report became arbitrary code execution this year; the patch took four engineers two months, and the thread's argument is that the asymmetry — not the bug — is the story.

Security★ 1,222 pts · ▲ +965 since the last edition · 714 comments

NEW YORK — The bug at the center of the front page's most-discussed thread was reported to the PHP Foundation as far back as 2024 and left alone as a mere crash: an out-of-memory error, unpleasant but presumed harmless. This year the security firm Calif, auditing PHP under OpenAI sponsorship, used AI tools to work out the exploit path anyway, turning the crash into a way to run arbitrary code on the host server. A patch shipped before any known exploitation.[1]

The thread has grown from 257 to 1,222 points since yesterday afternoon's coverage, and its center of gravity is the asymmetry the article documents: bug-finding is getting cheap — Columbia's Suman Jana calls it "a lot of very specific reasoning that is painful to do," which AI now does well — while the patch took four PHP Foundation engineers two months, because a fix must close every path without breaking anything. Jana points to formal verification, including an Anthropic release on 22 October that generates both patch and machine-checked proof, as the counterweight.[1][2]

Two independent notes of consequence. Johns Hopkins' Matthew Green: "Nobody's going to fix this problem until something really bad happens... PHP2Shell wasn't bad enough to be really bad." And OpenSSH's release notes, published today, report a large number of AI-assisted security reports and bugs independently rediscovered by others — the project will now ship fixes more frequently rather than batch them. "We've patched more bugs and more vulnerabilities in the last 3 months than entire years at my last job," one reader wrote.[3][2]

28 proxy services offered exits through the White House's network

Layer3 Intel caught the exits by chance while testing global rotating access; KrebsOnSecurity traces one service to a founder whose old hosting firm anchored the 2016 election intrusions.

Security★ 1,168 pts · ▲ +931 since the last edition · 378 comments

Staff devices on the Executive Office of the President's network (AS2650) beaconed to residential proxy services from October 5 to November 3, with 236 observed exits from six internet addresses on 26 of 30 days, mostly between 5 p.m. and midnight. Layer3 Intel reported the findings; its post sits behind a bot wall, and the detail here follows KrebsOnSecurity's independent report.[4][5]

Twenty-eight separate proxy services could exit through the network. Synthient traced the activity to multiple Windows 11 installations of Infatica, a program often bundled into free apps; its founder Vladimir Fomenko's earlier hosting firm, King Servers, carried six of the eight addresses Russia's services used against Arizona and Illinois election systems in 2016. Synthient's Ben Brundage: "Some of these resellers sell bandwidth for as low as 60 cents a Gigabyte... for less than a dollar you can gain access into some of the most confidential and trusted networks."[4]

Tools Desk · Bug Report

Same-day OpenSSH fix closes a musl password bypass ten years in the open

Accounts locked with an asterisk accept any 257–1,024-character password when sshd runs on musl without PAM — and Alpine's default cloud user has passwordless doas.

Tools★ 456 pts · 107 cmts

A single-character collision sits under today's OpenSSH release. When Alpine locked root's password with * in 2022 — restoring SSH key logins lost to an earlier ! fix — it met musl's 2012 decision to return * from crypt() on failure, and a 256-byte password cap added twenty days later. On sshd built with musl and without PAM, the sshhole.com writeup shows, an account whose password field is * will accept any password between 257 and 1,024 characters: past the cap, crypt fails, returns the star, and the stars match.[7]

OpenSSH 10.6p2, released today, carries "a critical security workaround" for the password authentication bypass on Linux systems using musl with PAM disabled, credited to Özgün Kültekin of Wakefield — whose firm built the writeup. The notes add that the project is receiving a large number of AI-assisted security reports and will release more frequently as a result.[3]

Language Desk

encoding/json/v2 is stable in Go 1.27

Six years after the problems were catalogued, Go's new JSON API rejects duplicate keys and invalid UTF-8 by default.

Language★ 351 pts · 178 cmts

The Go blog announces that encoding/json/v2 and encoding/json/jsontext are generally available in Go 1.27, after a year as an experiment. The new API reports errors for invalid UTF-8 and duplicate object member names by default — the latter a real attack surface, exploited before as CVE-2017-12635 — marshals nil slices and maps as empty arrays and objects rather than null, and replaces case-insensitive field matching with exact matches.[9]

Migration is the thread's theme: MarshalIndent has no direct equivalent and the replacement code is, one reader writes, "super complicated and non-obvious." Another marked the distance traveled: "The fact that this took 6 years to land stood out to me." A third, on the careful rollout: "This is how you roll out features, C++."[10]

Science Desk

Insects don't swarm at LED lights; the why is being rewritten

A 2016 Bristol field study holds up — LEDs drew significantly fewer insects. The UV explanation for it does not, says the author herself.

Science★ 294 pts · 145 cmts

A birding blog's account of why LEDs draw fewer insects — a Veracruz guide's geckos lost their food supply after his family switched lamps — rests on a University of Bristol field comparison of four domestic light types: LEDs attracted significantly fewer aerial insects than incandescent or compact fluorescent, with no significant difference between cool-white and warm-white.[12]

The mechanism in the post — insects cue on ultraviolet, which incandescents emit and LEDs do not — is the part under correction: the author has posted a notice that readers persuaded her she had the incandescent/UV issue backwards, and the Bristol paper itself concludes tungsten-filament bulbs are essentially UV-free, speculating their attraction may be thermal. HN readers added field reports of moth-free LED porches and a pet theory blaming PWM flicker.[12][13]

Security Desk · OpenAI

One AI run patched 948 Linux privilege-escalation vulnerabilities

OpenAI puts the API cost at $1.2 million and calls an unpatched low-privileged account "functionally equivalent to root" for a well-resourced attacker.

Security★ 419 pts · 304 cmts

OpenAI's security team reports an autonomous discovery, exploitation and patching pipeline that validated and generated patches for 948 local privilege escalation vulnerabilities in Linux in a single run with its cyber model, at an API cost its authors put at $1.2 million. The page blocks automated fetching; the figures are as quoted in the thread by the team and readers.[14][15]

As quoted in the thread, the report's threat model is blunt: a low-privileged account on an operating system not up to date on patches "should be considered functionally equivalent to root access for a well-resourced threat actor." A team member answered questions there; the top discussion asks how a thousand kernel escalations accumulated at all, and one reader reflected that few open-source projects could spend that much on security.[15]

AI Desk · Release

Ripple-65B claims the U.S. open-weight lead for $32 million

80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1 from 1.4 million RL rollouts — roughly 70× fewer than the prior mark, its maker says. The claim's scope is the thread's argument.

AI★ 313 pts · 111 cmts

Ricursive Intelligence released Ripple-65B Preview, a dense 65-billion-parameter model pretrained on 12 trillion tokens with 1M-token context, reporting 80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1 — above Reflection Beam 501B (80.1), Meta Muse Spark Open 816B (76.4) and NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B (56.4) among U.S. open-weight models, at a full from-scratch R&D cost the company puts at $32 million.[18]

The recipe is compute-frugal: 1.4 million RL rollouts — about 70× fewer than the prior open-weight SOTA, the company says — no purchased post-training data, and a thin-and-deep architecture that runs on an NVIDIA DGX Spark. The thread pushed on the claim's scope: Alibaba's Qwen 3.8 Flash Next scores 84.3 on the same benchmark, and the comparison chart names no non-U.S. models. "Category narrowing to this degree really has diluted the term 'SOTA' quite a lot," one reader wrote.[18][19]
