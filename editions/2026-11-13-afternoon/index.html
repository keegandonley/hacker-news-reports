Field Desk · Aviation

FAA orders a national ground stop of every U.S. airport, and says only that circumstances were "extraordinary"

Advisory 111, still posted at this paper's 22:00 UTC snapshot, halts all departing traffic from 20:54 UTC; the command center reports no information yet on the timing or cause of the outage behind it

Field Desk★ 453 pts · 182 comments

WARRENTON, VIRGINIA — The Federal Aviation Administration's Air Traffic Control System Command Center issued a national ground stop on Friday evening, ordering every U.S. airport to hold all departing traffic in an event window running from 20:53 UTC on Nov. 13 to 04:00 UTC on Nov. 14.[1] The advisory's stated grounds are nine words long: "DUE TO EXTRAORDINARY CIRCUMSTANCES AND FOR REASONS OF SAFETY," with all departing aircraft instructed to remain stopped "until further notice" and inbound international flights "managed on a case by case basis."[1] At this paper's snapshot, taken three minutes before 22:00 UTC, the advisory was still live on the FAA's operations page.[1]

What failed is not yet official. Transport Secretary Sean Duffy, quoted by the BBC's live page from the thread, said the stop followed "a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers."[2] The Air Current, quoting the FAA command center via Bloomberg, reported the stop was "due to several impacts to ATC Frequencies and/or Radar capabilities" — and that the command center "does not have further information on the 'timing or cause of this outage.'"[2] A pilot in the thread reported his captain "very confused" at the hold; another reader, watching final approach into Boston Logan on a flight tracker, counted arrivals still landing — consistent with an advisory that stops departures while managing arrivals case by case.[2]

The thread's licensed wing supplied the plumbing: controller microphones no longer run on a long cord to the antenna, one reader noted — centers reach remote transmitters through third-party telecom relays, a chain with outages before, most recently in September.[2] Another put the stakes plainly: this is "a system that's supposed to have its own built in redundancy and must not go down."[2]

"ALL DEPARTING TRAFFIC SHOULD REMAIN GROUND STOPPED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE."— ATCSCC Advisory 111, Nov. 13, 2026[1]
Culture Desk

LibreOffice posts "actually works 365 days a year," and the front page makes it 752 points

Culture Desk★ 752 pts · 237 cmts

The Document Foundation's LibreOffice account posted four words to X at 11:35 a.m. Friday — "LibreOffice actually works 365 days a year. Just saying" — into the second day of the Microsoft 365 outage this paper has covered since Thursday.[3] The post drew 1.1 million views and 64,000 likes by evening, and its Hacker News thread finished the day as the highest-scoring story on the page.[3][4]

The thread's most-replied comment took the other side: "If LibreOffice were good enough already, it would already be popular," arguing the project needs a Blender-style interface overhaul rather than a victory lap.[4] Others logged the costs the tweet leaves out — a four-to-five-second cold start on Windows, an Arch upgrade that left the suite unlaunchable for three days — while one reader recalled OpenOffice refusing to print on Tuesdays, a bug traced to a "Tue" string in its PostScript header.[4] A dissenting note: offline Microsoft Office "is and has been just fine," including a 2024 release updated this week.[4]

Hardware Desk · Reverse Engineering

A Wi-Fi air purifier gives up its cloud: the root certificate arrives over plain HTTP

Hardware Desk★ 615 pts · 182 cmts

Scott, posting as scottjg, wanted his discontinued Coway Airmega IconS purifier on his Apple Home "Good Night" button without a round trip through Seoul — the existing Home Assistant integration polls Coway's IoCare cloud, which broke at least twice a year, banned his account once, and rebranded itself from IoCare to IoCare+.[5] Two things changed, he writes: October's eMudhra certificate-authority incident left "anyone" holding a root CA — and mitmproxy now ships a flag for exactly that — and a language model would configure the whole man-in-the-middle rig, nftables and all, in minutes.[5]

The interception found the fan bootstrapping trust in the open: it fetches Amazon's Root CA 1 from an S3 bucket over plain HTTP, keeps no roots of its own, and pins nothing — so the eMudhra shortcut was never needed. "It was always MitM-able," Scott writes.[5] Control rides MQTT over TLS on port 8883 to AWS IoT in ap-northeast-2, and a single provisioning response hands the device its keys and its broker — control that response and the fan will talk to your server instead.[5]

The thread split three ways: one reader watching "this wave of LLM reverse engineering" marvel that the plain-HTTP bootstrap "was lying in plain sight" for anyone with the patience; a second asking why an air purifier needs Wi-Fi at all ("why allow it to occupy this much mental space for you?"); and a third arguing the purifier market is designed wrong way round, custom HEPA filters and all — a Corsi-Rosenthal box fan does more for less.[6]

Science Desk · From the Archive

The 1960 paper that set doomsday for today — by fitting a curve to population

Science Desk★ 407 pts · 283 cmts

Sixty-six years ago this week, Science published "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026" — Heinz von Foerster, Patricia M. Mora and Lawrence W. Amiot, Nov. 4, 1960, volume 132, pages 1291–1295 — which fit the available population series and carried it toward infinity on a date the arithmetic fixed: today.[7] The paper's point was the fit, not the forecast: a smoothly growing curve carried forward will blow up on a fixed date, and the date simply falls where the arithmetic puts it.[8]

The article itself sits behind Science's bot wall; this account is set from the journal's own bibliographic record and the thread, which spent its 283 comments — the most discussion on today's page — exactly where the paper points.[7] "Beautifully shows how most predictions that extrapolate from past data are too simplistic," wrote the most-replied comment, noting industrialized nations now fear decline more than growth.[8] One reply turned the lesson on the reply itself — don't extrapolate to superintelligence from this either — and another compressed the whole file: "People don't understand that a sigmoid looks like an exponential growth to infinity until it doesn't."[8]

One reader pointed to the paper's long afterlife in Adam Becker's book on Silicon Valley's apocalyptic extrapolations, "More Everything Forever"; another filed the date itself — a Friday the 13th in A.D. 2026, computed in 1960.[8]

Sixteen steps to open a slide deck, counted by a teacher

Field Desk★ 188 pts · 94 cmts

A teacher on r/Teachers counted what it takes a student to open one PowerPoint on a school iPad: log in to Clever, pick a Google profile, type a district email in the form "32lastnamefirstinitial@schoolname.k12.state.us" and its password, open Canvas, log in via Google again, pick the same profile again, open the class, open Modules, scroll past roughly 40 similarly named links, download the file, hand it to Keynote because PowerPoint isn't installed, turn the iPad vertically to reveal the Open button, unlock the screen, press Open, press Play, and finally "present on this device." Then, the post ends: "Take notes."[9]

Reddit blocked this paper's fetch; the steps are set from the full list quoted in the thread, where the top corrections went to the headline's attribution: on a stock iPad, tapping a .ppt file opens it — the sixteen steps belong to the school's stack, chiefly Clever's single sign-on sitting in front of Google, and Canvas on top of both.[9][10] Parents and teachers piled on with their own districts' versions — dropped keyboard cases, assignments scattered across Classroom and paper — and one reader noted Clever's "free for schools" model charges vendors for connections, with contract terms barring them from saying so.[10]

Culture Desk · Comment

A blogger counts the cost of a two-day outage, and the thread declines to blame the vibes

Culture Desk★ 128 pts · 100 cmts

"Big tech is here to let us down," James Sweeting wrote Friday, taking the Microsoft 365 outage as the text: workers celebrating a "snow day for adults," schools, courts and support desks dark, and no compensation expected — from a company, he argues, that fired tens of thousands including many engineers, and so made both the failure and the fix slower.[11] His prescription is structural: European state institutions are already moving off Microsoft, and the Global North's dependence is the vulnerability.[11]

The thread's most-replied comment ran past the essay's sharpest jab — that the outage might be "someone vibecoding their way via Copilot" — to call that habit itself lazy: "Blaming every downtime on 'vibecoding, probably' is so weird and lazy. As if these places never had downtime previously."[12] Others filed the week's other outages — an Amazon availability zone, a Google zone whose redundancies failed to start — against the "too big to go down" reasoning that skipped redundancy, and one reader noted the EternalBlue precedent for expecting no compensation at all.[12]

Security Desk · Cargo

$11.4 million in hardware left Oakland in August and never reached Texas

Security Desk★ 173 pts · 98 cmts

Six pallets of computer hardware with a reported value of $11.4 million were collected in Oakland on Aug. 29 for delivery to Fort Worth, on a truck displaying a food-transport company's logo; after several multi-hour stops, investigators lost its trail in San Bernardino County, FreightWaves reports from Mercury News reporting.[13] The Oakland Police Department confirmed an ongoing grand-theft investigation opened on a complaint filed Sept. 13, 2026.[13]

The wire's own caveats are the story's fine print: police have not confirmed the $11.4 million figure, identified the merchandise, the owner, the shipper, the carrier or the driver, made arrests, or announced any recovery — and the Sept. 13 complaint date does not establish when the theft occurred.[13] The report places the loss amid what it counts as nearly $111 million in stolen data-center freight, and follows more than $7.5 million in Nvidia-product burglaries investigated in Fremont and Union City during 2025; a separate September case saw two PlusAI trailers stolen in Fremont, one branded Nvidia, containing about 40,000 pounds of sand.[13]

The thread asked the basic questions: whether $11.4 million is even a lot ("I have no clue how much stuff 'vanishes' regularly," one reader admitted), why hardware of this value moves on ordinary couriers rather than secure logistics, and where the enforcement is — one reader linking coverage of gear "often ending up in China" and asking, flatly, "Hello, where is the FBI?"[14]

The Science Desk
Science Desk · AI & Archives

Claude reads Gödel's 1,009 shorthand pages and finds 663 theorems nobody knew

Science Desk★ 258 pts · 75 cmts

Kurt Gödel's workbooks — 16 notebooks, 1,009 pages from the early 1940s, much of it in Gabelsberger shorthand obsolete even then — yielded to a language model in September, The Guardian reports. Jan von Plato of the University of Helsinki had been reading them since 2017, at roughly a page a week; within days of showing Claude his transcriptions and six books of prior scholarship, he had "a 650-page explanation of the workbooks."[15]

Asked to extract every theorem Gödel had proved, sketched or gestured at, Claude found 663. Run through the Coq proof assistant: about 400 verified as stated, roughly 200 were repaired — a statement sharpened, an assumption added — and about 50 were false, which the marginalia anticipated; Gödel himself re-read his notes and marked them "falsch!" or "schlecht!"[15] The prize find is a near-complete proof of the independence of the axiom of choice — Gödel claimed it in 1966, when Paul Cohen won the Fields Medal for it, but never reconstructed it. Claude's version came within one wrong definition; von Plato ran it through Coq and it passed.[15]

Notre Dame logician Joel David Hamkins, who has not seen the reconstruction, supplied the discipline's ledger: "it is remarkable and historically interesting. Although how much is Claude and how much is Gödel is a job for the historians... You don't get credit for work you don't tell anyone about."[15] The thread noted the paper's own irony — von Plato learned one of the world's six readable shorthands to make himself indispensable, then made the reading machine-replaceable — and one historian's caution that Gödel published only bulletproof work.[16]

Language Desk · Ecosystem

Haskell's foundation ships a technical vision: builds from six hours to two

Language Desk★ 153 pts · 73 cmts

The Haskell Foundation's new technical leadership committee, bootstrapped after weeks of closed discussion, published its vision Thursday: "Imagine Haskell had the iteration speed of golang, the debugging capabilities of C# and the onboarding experience of rust. And all that while providing stronger guarantees and better performance!"[17] The concrete benchmark is build time — GHC's own CI turnaround runs six hours, and the committee wants it at two — alongside deterministic debugging "instead of helplessly dumping logs and binaries into LLM chats," the fastest incident response of any language ecosystem, regular penetration tests, and a stability target of compiling ten-year-old code on the latest GHC without source changes.[17]

The thread tested the pitch where it lives. One reader asked why a terse, strict, token-efficient language isn't more popular with AI agents — a question the vision itself half-answers by claiming Haskell as "where we maintain control of our work," the human-in-the-loop language.[18] Another pasted a five-argument Haskell type signature from Gemini and reported where onboarding really fails: "My brain just crashes after the '::'."[18] A third argued the Rust comparison cuts the other way — Cargo succeeds precisely because it does not do one thing.[18]

AI Desk · Research

Six hundred experiments on a world model, and the wins are in the planner, not the predictions

AI Desk · Research★ 169 pts · 30 cmts

Haddy Alchaer ran 600 experiments on LeWM, the JEPA world model from a team including Yann LeCun, on the Push-T robot task — and reports that as published it solves 22 percent of long-horizon tasks (goal 75 steps away), while the same model given a subgoal every 25 steps solves 80 percent. Hierarchical planning was worth nearly 60 points; swapping in the simulator's perfect physics was worth 20.[19]

The post builds a memory graph from 460,000 embedded demonstration frames — a jump cost between demonstrations lifts it from 62 to 85 percent — then shows the standard benchmark flatters everyone: for 84 percent of its goals the graph already held a near-identical state, and 38 of 250 episodes flip on the planner's random seed alone. A last finding is the quiet one: scoring the robot pusher's position in the goal image, rather than only the block's, took exact-physics success from 39 of 50 tasks to 9. "A split like that happens by chance about twice in a billion," Alchaer writes — the 4x came from how the goal was made, not the model.[19] The thread's sharpest reply asked for less "discovering" and more citation — hierarchical planning and sub-goals "are well understood... this is more of a transcript of your personal learning journey" — and the author answered questions in-thread.[20]

Corrections & Clarifications

"National ground stop" stops departures, not all flight. The thread largely read as everything halted; Advisory 111 orders "ALL DEPARTING TRAFFIC" held, manages inbound international flights "on a case by case basis," and a reader watching Boston Logan counted arrivals still landing throughout.[1][2]

The sixteen clicks are mostly not the iPad's. On a stock iPad, tapping a PowerPoint file opens it, several readers with the hardware confirmed; the count belongs to the school's stack — Clever sign-on, Google login, Canvas, and a Keynote handoff because PowerPoint isn't installed.[10]

"Hundreds of proved theorems" includes the repaired. Of 663 theorems Claude extracted, about 400 verified in Coq as stated, roughly 200 needed repair before proving, and about 50 were false — with the caveat, from Notre Dame's Hamkins, that "how much is Claude and how much is Gödel is a job for the historians."[15]

The $11.4 million is a reported figure, not a finding. Police have not confirmed the valuation or identified the merchandise; the complaint dates to Sept. 13 for an Aug. 29 pickup — the loss is ten weeks old and newly reported, not new.[13]
