AI agents find a Maya site in 72 hours — and miss that archaeologists mapped it in 2025

Forty-eight agents working public NASA lidar flagged a hilltop complex Mexico's register did not hold; the published literature did

From the AI Desk★ 880 pts · 318 comments

MAYA LOWLANDS, MEXICO — A developer who set 48 AI agents loose on roughly 1,200 square kilometres of public NASA lidar strips reports that, in under 72 hours, they flagged an unregistered hilltop site — a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid at least 8–12 metres tall, a raised courtyard, and a flat-bottomed hollow that may be a reservoir — and prepared the paperwork: a report, translated into Spanish by the agents, sent to Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH).[1] INAH replied the next day that its records held no registered archaeological site at that location, according to a diary of the run that begins 18 October, while the author was travelling in Peru, and ends with the government reply on 21 October.[1]

The method had no classifier behind it. The agents converted lidar strips into images and reviewed them, in the author's phrase, "like very patient archaeologists," filtering out natural hills, sinkholes and roads while hunting for pyramids, platforms and plazas; they then re-checked the raw point cloud to confirm the shapes sat on the ground rather than in the canopy.[1] The speculation, flagged as such in the post, is a small Classic-period civic centre (roughly AD 250–900).[1]

The correction arrived on 11 November, in an update to the post itself: the site was already mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025, and the agents missed it while searching the literature. What the run actually found was a site absent from INAH's register, not from archaeology — "still work to do then!" the author wrote.[1] The thread had been pushing on exactly that seam: one reader waited for "an expert archaeologist's verification," having watched agents produce findings that "completely falters on further review," while another noted the real test is whether any of the structure identifications survive ground work.[2] The author's own takeaway: "You can just do things. I'm not an archaeologist, so I didn't know this was supposed to be hard."[1]

Language Desk

Python mourns Tim Peters, author of the Zen of Python and Timsort

Language Desk★ 653 pts · 64 cmts

Guido van Rossum announced on the Python Discourse that Tim Peters died earlier this year, on 15 July.[3] The memorial post credits Peters with the Zen of Python (PEP 20), still printed by import this, and with Timsort, the list-sorting algorithm he designed for Python in 2002 that Java and Android later adopted.[3]

Peters found Python soon after its first release; his first contribution, van Rossum writes, was arguing that the language should support mixed arithmetic between ints and floats because users would expect it. On the early mailing lists he answered so many questions, so carefully, that he earned the nickname "Timbot" — replies marked with <wink>, and sometimes described as "channeling Guido." Before Python, he wrote compilers for Cray Research and speech-to-text software at Dragon Systems.[3]

Van Rossum's sign-off: "Python-would-not-be-what-it-is-today-without-Tim-ly y'rs."[3] On the Hacker News thread, readers introduced him the shortest way available — "The Tim of Tim Sort" — and posted the full Zen in his memory; one wrote of hoping newer Python users keep it alive "in an agentic era."[4]

"He never treated you like an idiot, never dismissed your concerns out of hand, always answered and explained."— a reply in the Python memorial thread
Cloud Desk

Amazon open-sources an AWS API emulator it says agents wrote entirely

Cloud Desk★ 480 pts · 162 cmts

Amazon has open-sourced AWS Emulator, a local program that serves AWS API endpoints so software can be built and tested without calling AWS or holding an AWS account; it ships with no additional service fee, and Amazon's announcement describes the code as "100% written by autonomous AI agents," thousands of them, writing tests and fixing code without human review.[5][6]

It runs in server mode — point an SDK or the CLI at localhost:9297 over a self-signed certificate — or in embedded mode as a Rust dev-dependency spun up per test, and an agent skill installs it via the AWS CLI. Fidelity varies by service: scores ship with each release, and gaps are tracked in an issues/ directory split between "things that do not work" and "things that now work."[5]

The thread took the announcement's provenance seriously and skeptically: one reader measured the checkout at 2.2 GB — 1.3 GB of it .git — and asked whether the agent-built method "is more of a sly marketing stunt or a real indication that this methodology is practical and economical."[7] Others said they would wait to see whether Amazon maintains it before building on it, pointing at Testcontainers-based alternatives.[7]

Field Desk

A false smoke alarm nearly drowned Jane Street's only NYC datacenter

Field Desk★ 334 pts · 166 cmts

A systems engineer's firsthand account details the night construction dust tripped the smoke detector in Jane Street's New York datacenter — a machine room the trading firm kept on the 33rd floor of 1 New York Plaza, across the hall from its trading floor, so that out-of-control systems could always be stopped by pulling power cables.[8] The alarm was wired to shut down the CRAC cooling units, to keep air from feeding any fire — which left a room full of racks climbing in temperature while the sprinkler lines above them pressurized.[8]

The author, who lived a nine-minute walk away, ran in and pulled the power on every device, cabinet by cabinet, watching the monitor climb: 141°F, 143, 143.5, 143.6 — then stop, short of the roughly-150°F heat plugs that would have opened the sprinklers onto the floor.[8] New York's markets were closed for the day; the firm was trading again the next morning.[8]

The post's institutional punchline is an OCaml daemon that asks every Linux server for its onboard temperature each minute and alarms on the fleet-wide mean — chosen because per-server heat is routine and CRAC telemetry proved less trustworthy than the machines themselves.[8] The office datacenter survived the review that followed; Hurricane Sandy ended it.[8] The thread contributed its own proximity-to-work war stories, and one reader's question about why a machine room had sprinklers at all rather than gas suppression drew the author's reply that other Jane Street rooms had it.[9]

Hardware Desk

Voltage glitching turns a mining card into an A100 by blowing fuses

Hardware Desk★ 238 pts · 98 cmts

A researcher reports a voltage-fault-injection vulnerability on NVIDIA's GA100 die that allows an operator to blow arbitrary fuses — and used it to convert a CMP 170HX 8 GB mining card into what the post calls "effectively an A100-80G": 81,920 MiB reported, 124 compute units at 1410 MHz, and roughly 1.2× the throughput of a retail A100, whose yield-safe configuration ships with 108 SMs.[10] The trick works because several GA100 restrictions relax, rather than tighten, when more fuses are blown — the inverse of the usual assumption that a blown fuse only removes capability.[10]

The disclosure was coordinated: the findings went to NVIDIA's product security team ahead of publication, and the post went out after an independent researcher found the same vulnerability.[10] CMP-disabled features including NVLink and PCIe Gen4 are unlockable by the same route; NVJPG, NVDEC and MIG were not tested.[10]

The thread's sharpest objection is yield: many cut-down GA100 parts are cut down because fabrication faults disabled silicon, so a re-fused die may carry defects NVIDIA's own testing caught — though several readers countered that late-run yields are often good enough that segmentation, not salvage, decides the SKU.[11]

Graphics Desk

Hyper-realistic sand runs grain by grain in the browser

Graphics Desk★ 255 pts · 57 cmts

A WebGL2 and JavaScript sand simulator runs in the browser on phones, tablets and desktops: drag to push grains, tilt the phone to slide them, shake to scatter them, per the project page.[12] The page is otherwise nearly wordless — the toy is the documentation — and it topped 255 points on a day of heavyweight competition.[12]

The thread's practical findings: it works best with the phone lying flat, screen up, with orientation locked — otherwise every tilt resets the layout — and it is a sandbox, not a falling-sand game. "It took me a while to realize this isn't a falling sand (Powder Toy) type game," one reader wrote; several reported the pour simply looked wrong until the device was flat.[13]

The Science Desk
Research · Model Internals

An eight-hour LLM weight edit turns out to be a linear program

Science Desk★ 219 pts · 33 cmts

A new analysis of MIT CSAIL's "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" — in which GPT-6 Astra edited 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights and lifted MATH-500 accuracy from 7.9% to 48.1% — finds the celebrated edit is mechanically a rank-1 push against the residual stream's "loop direction": Astra spent about 8 hours and 28 forward passes finding what two linear-program solves of roughly a minute each reproduce with 8× fewer changed weights.[14]

The post works the mechanism: Qwen3.5-0.8B loops, 93% of its base MATH-500 answers hitting the 32,768-token limit; the edit subtracts each column's component along the loop direction in two layers, and choosing which columns and how hard is exactly a sparse LP.[14] A plain inference-time steering vector at the right strength recovers "essentially the whole fix" — 57.8 on the dev set against the LP edit's 62.5 and the base model's 10.9 — with a narrow window: half strength and the loops return, double and reasoning collapses.[14] The thread's shortest gloss: what took a frontier model 20.3 hours is "a linear optimization algorithm from the 01940s editing a Qwen model with billions of parameters."[15]

Research · Policy

Essay: the null results pile argues for institutionalized humility

Science Desk★ 168 pts · 99 cmts

Experimental History's latest essay proposes — with a straight face carried mostly by the title — that policy makers sacrifice a goat before launching major programs, the point being "procedurally-induced humility": a ritual reminder that we know nothing, because an alarming share of well-intentioned interventions do not work.[16]

The evidence table is the substance: $3 billion in 2009 School Improvement Grants with no detectable effect; chief diversity officers hired 2001–2019 with no measured diversity change; a $280 million Afghan jobs program whose results cannot be stated; a 10-year RCT across 531 Peruvian schools finding zero academic effect of One Laptop Per Child; and one of four pro-vaccine messages that accidentally increased parents' belief vaccines cause autism.[16] The essay's frame is sociologist Peter Rossi's 1987 "Iron Law of Evaluation" — the net expected impact of a large social program is zero — and the thread argued over whether such programs exist to work or to be seen working.[16][17]

Atuin syncs agent sessions across machines and harnesses

Practical Tech Corner★ 168 pts · 62 cmts

Atuin 18.24 adds Threads: one searchable, synchronized history for agent sessions across machines — start a task in Claude Code on a dev server, resume it in Codex on a laptop, with the full transcript attached.[18] Sync is end-to-end encrypted when enabled, over a hosted server, a self-hosted one, or fully local; capturing agent sessions is opt-in, and supported harnesses are Claude Code, Codex, Pi, omp and OpenCode.[18]

An MCP server, installed as atuin mcp, lets later agents search those sessions themselves — the post's worked example is a triage agent that finds a prior investigation of a misconfigured object-store key and fixes it without redoing the diagnosis.[18] The thread split on the risk model: one reader has spent months sandboxing agents from each other and scanning transcripts for leaked credentials, so a unified session store is precisely what he avoids; another answered that the self-hosted backend removes the need to trust anyone.[19]

AI · APIs

Google's Decisions API scores 1,000 questions in one pass

Practical Tech Corner★ 141 pts · 45 cmts

Google DeepMind introduced the Gemini Decisions API with a new model, gemini-decision-1: pass one multimodal input — text, images, audio, video or PDFs, up to 256K tokens — plus up to 1,000 independent yes/no, multiple-choice (2–255 options) or ordered-rubric questions, and receive probabilities and confidence scores in a single forward pass rather than token-by-token text.[20]

Quoted numbers: 150–250 ms end-to-end latency, $0.10 per million input tokens, zero output tokens billed, and — on Google's own benchmark suite — nearly 5× cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the Interactions API.[20] The thread read it as Google shipping its own decision-model API and noted audio and video input as the differentiator, alongside the usual wariness about how long any given Google endpoint stays alive.[21]

Corrections & Clarifications

Unmapped, unregistered. The Maya post's headline says the agents found an "unmapped" site; the author's own 11 November update states the site was mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025 and that his agents missed it in the literature search. What was missing was the INAH register entry, not the mapping.[1]

"Effectively an A100-80G." The glitched CMP 170HX reports 124 compute units against a retail A100's yield-safe 108, and its NVJPG, NVDEC and MIG unlocks were not tested — the equivalence claim rests on nvidia-smi output and benchmarks the author ran, on a die re-fused outside NVIDIA's test coverage.[10]

Nearly 5× cheaper, by one comparison. Google's Decisions API pricing claim is measured against Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the Interactions API, on Google's own benchmark suite, and counts zero billed output tokens; it is not a general cheaper-than-Gemini claim.[20]

"100% written by autonomous AI agents." Amazon's phrase describes authorship of AWS Emulator's code; the emulator's fidelity to AWS APIs varies by service, per the repository's own fidelity scores and issues tracker, and the README bars production workloads and security testing outright.[5][6]
