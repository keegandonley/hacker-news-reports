Number theorist claims a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, written with Claude and formalized in Lean

Levent Alpöge’s four-page note says the formalization is finished and released in fourteen parts; the thread calls it the biggest day in math in recent memory

Science Desk★ 2132 pts · 1457 comments †

SAN FRANCISCO — Levent Alpöge, a number theorist who joined Anthropic as a member of the technical staff after a Harvard Society of Fellows junior fellowship and a Princeton doctorate under Manjul Bhargava, has posted a four-page note claiming a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, the 1859 conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers.[1][3] “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” the note opens, adding that “the method easily extends much further,” resolving the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and, he expects, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. The formalization, he writes, is finished and available as fourteen downloadable parts.[1]

The argument descends from Stepanov’s function-field method: iterated theta lifts between totally definite unitary groups, with the chain length chosen as √X for a von Mangoldt sum of length X (“And yet, what else could it be?”), an Iwasawa tower, and a Rallis-style period argument, with divisibility handled through the Chowla–Selberg, Weyl and Siegel mass formulas. “No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line,” Alpöge writes of the closing stretch. Terence Tao, posting on the same results the same day, says the proof “appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry,” and that checking it “will take some time.”[1][16] On the timing — the note appeared the day after Anthropic’s Nasdaq debut — Alpöge writes that “the precise timing is horrible,” that he asked Anthropic’s chief executive whether he could announce (“Hypothetically, if I were to have . . .”), that “there was a constraint to respect,” and that the cynical reading of the timing is false.[1]

The thread made the day’s largest discussion. “This is Levent Alpöge . . . a supremely qualified mathematician and one of the best in the world at guiding AI towards new discoveries in mathematics,” one reader wrote: “If he says he has Riemann, well then, he has Riemann.” Another called it “probably the biggest day in math in recent memory,” and a third worried that proofs too long for any human to read move the work of understanding onto machines.[2]

A second Riemann draft — all human — is hurried out the same morning

Science Desk★ 1059 pts · 502 cmts

Ravi Vakil, posting from an American Mathematical Society Executive Committee and Board of Trustees meeting he was about to chair, hurried out word of a second claimed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis: Alessandro Masullo’s, checked closely for months by Brian Conrad, whose “extensive feedback . . . is being incorporated, and it is so far looking good.” “It is all human, with no AI,” Vakil writes. The plan had been to bring Masullo to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and announce after running it by enough people; “Now it seems best to put out the current draft,” with the file, titled “The Music Of The Primes,” linked from the post.[4]

“I hope people quickly check it, formalize, fill any small gaps if they occur,” Vakil writes. “I really hope it works. I have great faith in Brian’s judgement.” One reader counted from the draft that the work has been twelve years in the making.[4][5]

The thread read the timing directly: was the hurried release a response to Alpöge’s publication hours earlier, or did Alpöge know of Masullo’s near-complete work and release first? The draft itself states, in the words one reader quoted, that “[t]he math is complete.”[5]

The AI-designed CPU returns at 577 points, more than double its morning score

Hardware Desk★ 577 pts · ▲ +302 since the last edition · 307 cmts

Architect Labs’ Frostbite — a general-purpose CPU whose instruction set its AI systems “discovered, designed and verified end-to-end” — added 302 points since the morning edition to reach 577, the day’s third-largest story. The claim stands: 4.59× the performance-per-watt of a “modern, silicon-proven superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU core” as a geometric mean over 25 workloads, 15 of them held out until final evaluation, and 8.77× performance-per-area, with agents having iterated over the compiler, ISA, microarchitecture, RTL, verification and final GDS layout.[6]

The architecture runs interaction nets — every step local, every dependency explicit, every value with exactly one owner — with a work-stealing runtime the company says no human specified, a specification language that “lowered down to 800k+ lines of Lean,” a swarm that tried 58 ideas and kept 37, and a design synthesized on a 7 nm process at 800 MHz, 500 MHz in the final GDS. The comparison core is unnamed: “for legal reasons, we cannot disclose the exact CPU.”[6]

The afternoon thread kept pressing on scale: the chosen benchmarks “look small” — “json parsing with 4096 chars, mergesort with 1024 elements, fib(20)” — one reader wrote; another saw an old shape returning: “We will continue to invent Transputer-shaped things forever, it seems.” Wider benchmarking against out-of-order cores, on SPEC and PARSEC, is promised in the coming months.[7][6]

Feynman, asked for a freshman explanation, concluded: “That means we don’t really understand it”

Language Desk★ 572 pts · 277 cmts

A 2017 kottke.org post on explanation and understanding held the page’s fourth spot. Its center is an account from David Goodstein’s Feynman’s Lost Lecture: asked to “explain to me, so that I can understand it, why spin one-half particles obey Fermi-Dirac statistics,” Feynman offered to prepare a freshman lecture, then returned days later: “I couldn’t do it. I couldn’t reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don’t really understand it.” The post pairs it with John Gruber’s account of the same rule as an engineering practice at Apple, and with Feynman’s own surrenders on magnets and quantum mechanics: “I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics.”[8]

The thread read the nine-year-old post against the day’s news. “Everyone working on how Claude communicates would benefit from reading this,” one reader wrote; another kept the caveat, that “simple to me might not be simple to you,” and a third asked “explain to what level?” — noting that some things can be put in simple terms and still not be understood.[9]

Culture Desk

Google workers petition their employer not to cut schools’ Search for refusing Gemini

Culture Desk★ 209 pts · 144 cmts

Hundreds of Google workers are pushing senior leadership to retract a policy that would block schools that turn off its Gemini AI tools from Google Search starting next year, Mother Jones reports. Google made the announcement two weeks ago, on Election Day, saying it wanted to address teachers’ and parents’ concerns about AI in the classroom; since then, protests organized by a nationwide alliance of parent and community groups have been held in New York and six other cities, with signs reading “NO AI IN MY SCHOOL!” and “Stop stealing our imaginations.” Three of the country’s largest districts — New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago — are now discussing alternative search engines.[20]

The petition, brought by the Alphabet Workers Union, which represents more than 1,000 workers, is addressed to Sundar Pichai and the company’s education and search executives: “Any opportunity to drive positive change with AI in education will rely on educators having the freedom to choose how to implement it in their curriculum . . . Cutting off access to Search for schools that don’t wish to use AI will not help persuade our users, and will more likely result in them shutting out Google products altogether.” A software engineer in the education department said most workers had not known of the decision before it was announced publicly, and that leadership has acknowledged the letter without responding to its specific demands.[20]

The thread split over whether refusing AI and keeping Search is one purchase or two: “Google’s product is AI. It’s like schools refusing to buy JIF because it contains peanuts,” one reader wrote, while another argued the petition amounts to asking Google to maintain a second, AI-free version of its product, and that switching to a search engine without AI “would probably do more than protests.”[21]

Terence Tao sorts the day’s mathematics, in decreasing order of digestion

Science Desk★ 241 pts · 68 cmts

Terence Tao posted a field guide to the day’s three results, “in decreasing order of how ‘digested’ the results currently are.” First: Levent Alpöge, Tristan Buckmaster and Matei Coiculescu, “with heavy AI assistance,” have written a human-readable proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced incompressible Euler equations in three dimensions, formalized in Lean. Second: Alpöge and Buckmaster have a proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in three dimensions, with a Lean formalization — the strategy, building an approximate discretely self-similar solution numerically and converting it to an exact one by rigorous perturbation theory, follows one of the expected paths, “but more work needs to be done to create a proper, professionally written proof.” Third: Alpöge, “using an internal AI model,” has generated an “(enormous)” Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, whose verification can be followed on the Lean Zulip — where the moderators have now focused the discussion on the Lean-related aspects only.[16]

Tao expects the digestion to take some time, though “in this case at least we have a human author to communicate with.” The thread added a verification detail: “It looks like two independent kernels accept the result,” one reader wrote of the Lean thread, “which means its likely that there are no kernel bug exploitations in the proof.” Another reader noted a difference in method between the day’s claims — the Riemann proof appears generated directly in Lean, while the Navier–Stokes work reads like mathematics written to be read.[17]

Security Desk

Signal blocks its own screenshots to keep OS-level AI agents out of its messages

iOS 27 and detectable Android capture are covered; macOS is not, because — Signal says — Apple’s APIs will not allow it

Security Desk★ 428 pts · 272 cmts

Signal is “restricting the latest AI agents built into mobile operating systems from potentially capturing and storing your Signal data,” president Meredith Whittaker wrote: the app will block screenshots on iOS 27, and on Android will automatically enable its screen-security setting when it detects an AI assistant attempting to read the screen. The company took comparable measures against Microsoft’s Recall on Windows 11 last year.[10]

The post names the mechanism and the stakes: Google’s Gemini Intelligence and Apple’s Onscreen Awareness with Siri AI put agents deep into the operating system, where they “scan” what is on screen, and the captured data is then available “to whatever local database or remote server an OS-level AI agent is using at that moment” — outside Signal’s control once captured. “This is an existential threat to Signal,” Whittaker writes. The remedy is, by the post’s own account, incomplete: it does not address filesystem access, users can disable it, macOS is uncovered because the platform “cannot reliably block screenshots” there, and “Signal is the canary in the coal mine.”[10]

Readers pressed at the edges: one noted the protection cannot help when a contact’s device is what leaks the conversation; another proposed the platform-level fix, an “AI/agent” permission like location or photos; a third asked Signal to publish its forensics findings so developers with fewer resources could use them.[11]

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, a laptop-scale model that runs its middle layers three times

AI Desk★ 330 pts · 45 cmts

Inkling 2 Mini is a looped transformer — the middle layers of the 24-layer model run three times per token, applying more reasoning before each answer — with 26.4 billion total parameters, 3.4 billion active, and a memory footprint of about 20 GB that fits on consumer laptops. On a flight, the company says, it ran offline on a MacBook Pro and built a playable flight simulator in a few minutes. It leads every open-weights model under 100 billion total parameters on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and the Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%), and its weights are trained and deployed natively in NVFP4, with no conversion loss from quantization.[12]

A company note left in the thread’s linked discussion says quantizers have found the weights unusually robust: “folks like Unsloth have noticed that our models seem to respond very well to being quantized, maintaining 90% top-1 even at 1-bit quantization.” A variable-loop research variant is released alongside it — “I’m not aware of any other working open source models like that,” one reader wrote — and the company’s own limitations section puts Qwen3.8-27B ahead on multi-turn chat (MultiChallenge 50.0%, ComplexConstraints 33.4%).[13][12]

AI Desk

Researchers claim an open 27-billion-parameter model can rewrite its own weights and improve

AI Desk★ 290 pts · 97 cmts

A paper posted to arXiv on November 16 (revised November 18) by Jiajun Fan and ten co-authors claims “to our knowledge for the first time, a language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training.” An open 27-billion-parameter model, given its weights, graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its own failure and rewrites the weights behind it: the first edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw, it tests edits on practice problems and submits only what works, over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rises, and it repairs a damaged copy of itself to over 80% of the original’s accuracy on unseen image tasks. The ability, the paper says, emerges with scale — smaller models in the same family “make no edit or break themselves.”[14]

The thread asked where the gain comes from — one reader wondered whether the added fidelity ultimately derives from the model’s self-run practice problems, and another suspected overfitting by self-conversation — and noted what the paper, which calls self-rewriting “a necessary step toward recursive self-improvement,” leaves out: “‘Alignment’ is not mentioned once in this article.” A third reader pointed to Google’s published “sleep” framework, which also updates weights from self-generated practice examples, as prior art.[15]

Corrections & Clarifications

“Two proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis.” Neither is established. Alpöge’s is a claimed proof whose checking, in Tao’s words, “will take some time”; Masullo’s is a proposed proof “so far looking good” after months of Conrad’s scrutiny, with small gaps still to fill. Today both words name claims, not results.

Frostbite’s “4.59× performance-per-watt.” The multiple is a geometric mean against one dual-issue, in-order core of similar area — not a flagship out-of-order CPU — over 25 small workloads (JSON parsing of 4,096 characters and mergesort of 1,024 elements among them), and the baseline chip is unnamed “for legal reasons.” The figures are pre-silicon.

Signal “blocks AI agents.” The protection covers screenshots on iOS 27 and screen capture the app can detect on Android. By Signal’s own account it does not address filesystem access, cannot cover macOS, and can be switched off by the user. Two known capture paths, on two platforms, imperfectly.

Inkling 2 Mini “leads every open-weights model.” The lead holds on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and the Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%); on MultiChallenge (50.0%) and ComplexConstraints (33.4%) the company’s own table puts Qwen3.8-27B ahead, and its limitations section concedes it is “not the strongest small model for multi-turn chat.”
