Nvidia’s board reinstates Jensen Huang as chief executive, after “input from senior levels of the United States government”

A unanimous vote, ten days after a resignation over reported affairs — one with a woman the Times says contacted China’s Ministry of State Security — and a close of up 31 percent; at 1,707 points the day’s biggest story, and at 2,814 comments its biggest argument

Hardware Desk★ 1,707 pts · 2,814 cmts †

SANTA CLARA, CALIFORNIA — Nvidia’s board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive on Monday, effective immediately and by unanimous vote, ten days after he resigned over reports of several extramarital affairs — one of them, the New York Times reported Sunday, with a woman who was in contact with an officer of China’s Ministry of State Security during at least part of the relationship. The board said it acted after “a series of discussions and meetings” with Huang, that it “received input from senior levels of the United States government,” and that it is confident his return “poses no national security risk.”[1]

President Donald Trump told CNBC he had spoken with the board “directly and indirectly,” that Huang “did nothing wrong,” and that he had asked him on Sunday to promise to “work hard.” Shares closed up 31 percent, recovering most of the nearly 33 percent they had shed since Nov. 8, when Huang first disclosed an affair to employees; the stock had closed at $186.64 on Nov. 6. Colette Kress, the finance chief who served as interim chief executive and had said the board was not considering reinstatement, stays on as CFO. The independent committee the board formed — with the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz — to investigate whether company secrets or national security were compromised will “proceed to conclusion,” the board said, and Huang is “committed to cooperating with it.”[1]

The thread’s sharpest reads were on governance rather than the man. One reader compared the reversal to OpenAI’s 2023 — “a firm’s nominal governance structure isn’t necessarily its real governance structure” — and another fixed on the sentence the board itself put forward: the “input from senior levels of the United States government” line is “the most operative part of the statement.” A third took the precedent darkly: break the rules, stay valuable, stay protected. Elon Musk, Gavin Baker and Brad Gerstner had lobbied for the return; Musk wrote that the board’s “mistake has been corrected.”[2]

Tools Desk

Ladybird ships its first alpha: a browser with a new engine, downloadable today

Tools Desk★ 1,079 pts · 270 cmts

Ladybird, the browser project led by Andreas Kling, is now in alpha, with downloads for macOS and Linux. The engine is Ladybird’s own — “built from the ground up, and not a fork” — though the browser around it is, in Kling’s words, “modest, but functional.” The alpha’s caveats are stated as plainly as its pitch: some websites won’t work, and it will crash sometimes. Crash reports are readable inside the browser, and the reader decides whether to send one.[3]

The alpha is a rolling channel with automatic updates — “the part I am most excited about,” Kling writes, since until now you had to build from source to watch the browser improve. WebDriver and the Firefox DevTools protocol are both supported for testing your own sites. And the project is unusual in asking for trouble outright: “We want security nerds to hack it! A new browser gets hacked, over and over, and that’s part of the process, so we make no promises about security today. When you pwn us, please tell us how so we can harden Ladybird!”[3]

The thread split between celebration and nostalgia: one reader credited the project’s pace to swapping self-built libraries for production-grade ones like SQLite and Skia, while missing “the original hacker spirit” of its SerenityOS lineage; a tester reported that every site he threw at it rendered correctly, scrolling a little jerky; and one practical soul drew the alpha’s real line — fine for browsing, careful with “anything that doesn’t require login/auth/payments/downloads.”[4]

AI Desk

Yann LeCun accuses Anthropic’s chief of “hyping the dangers” of AI to outlaw open models and “stay in control”

AI Desk★ 711 pts · 405 cmts

Yann LeCun — NYU professor, ACM Turing Award laureate, and until this year Meta’s chief AI scientist, now founder of AMI Labs — posted a broadside on X on Sunday evening against Anthropic’s chief executive, Dario Amodei, whose congressional testimony on the danger of open-weight models the thread carried as its context. “Dario’s spiel since GPT-2 has always been about hyping the dangers so that open models would be made illegal and *he* would stay in control,” LeCun wrote. “He left OpenAI because he wasn’t going to have control. It’s like the Ottoman Empire banning the printing press for Arabic… It’s a new form of medieval obscurantism.” The post had drawn 1.3 million views by press time.[5]

The thread took the accusation seriously enough to game out the policy it fears. One reader who works in the regulatory field said the emerging consensus there is “if you can’t ban the bullets, then you ban the guns” — ideas floated range from requiring Nvidia and AMD to push microcode updates that limit consumer GPUs’ ability to run language models, to registering privately owned compute, “to even just requiring that GPUs are just outright surrendered.” Another compressed the whole complaint into three lines: “Appropriate the world’s knowledge. Wrap it up into a proprietary product built atop decades of open source work and published research. Lobby to prevent others from doing the same.”[6]

The thread’s more careful readers split the risk in two — misuse of AI as tools, which open weights lower, and loss of control over AI, which they raise — and one argued from the recent HuggingFace incident that without open models there is no way to defend against attacks from closed frontier ones.[6]

Field Desk · App Stores

Apple pulled a nine-year-old open-source notes app from every EU App Store, and has not answered its developer since Nov. 5

Field Desk★ 378 pts · 75 cmts

Oleksandr Hlushchenko, the independent Ukrainian developer behind the open-source notes app FSNotes, wrote Monday that Apple has removed his app from every European App Store — nine years after it first shipped. The trouble began, he says, when his October sales payout never arrived; he opened support cases on Nov. 5 and Nov. 17 and has received “not a single response.” He says he completed the compliance information App Store Connect requires under the EU’s Digital Services Act and submitted official documents from Ukraine’s government business registry, where he is a registered sole proprietor. “While Apple remains silent, everything I’ve spent nine years building is being destroyed” — rankings, search visibility, revenue. The code stays free on GitHub either way; without revenue, he writes, a project like this cannot survive.[7]

The thread found the likely explanation Apple never gave him: a cached copy of the Netherlands App Store listing reads “Oleksandr Hlushchenko has not identified himself as a merchant for this app” — verified-trader status, a DSA requirement that “can lapse for all kinds of reasons (such as an address changing),” with the app still on sale in the U.S. and UK stores. The reader who dug it up was blunt about both halves: “It sucks that Apple is unresponsive, but I think the author is leaving out some important information.” Another put the era in one line — it is “crazy that upvotes are now the de-facto entry barrier for official customer support.”[8]

Science Desk · Space

After eight years and 6 billion miles, BepiColombo swings into orbit around Mercury — only the second mission ever to circle it

Science Desk★ 251 pts · 80 cmts

After a voyage of more than eight years and 6 billion miles, the European–Japanese BepiColombo mission was set this week to fire its thrusters Saturday and swing into orbit around Mercury — the smallest planet, the first rock from the sun, and one that will have been circled by only two spacecraft in history. Getting there is mostly braking: because the planet sits near the center of the solar system, craft accelerate under the sun’s gravity and “must spend much of their time braking to avoid shooting straight past,” the Guardian’s science editor Ian Sample writes of the €1.6 billion mission.[9]

In early December the spacecraft separates into two orbiters: the European one to map the surface and its geochemistry and probe the interior — Mercury is only 50 percent larger than the moon but four times its mass, with an unusually large core — and the Japanese one to measure the magnetic field and the sodium exosphere, the thin shell of gas around the planet. Science operations begin in April. “We’re very excited, it’s been a long wait,” said Prof. Emma Bunce of the University of Leicester, who has worked on the project for twenty years and leads one of its instruments.[9]

The orbit is a lease, not a keeping: the craft are expected to circle Mercury for about three years, then run out of fuel, lose altitude, and slam into the surface — “Sadly, they are not coming back,” Bunce said. On the thread, one reader was still working out the insertion’s fine print — how serious was the anomaly that prevented the full orbital burn, and how good is the resulting orbit? — and another admitted surprise that an eight-year, two-nation mission to Mercury had flown so far under the radar next to NASA’s coverage.[10]

Culture Desk · Gaming

A full Xbox build of GTA 6 leaks, and the recompilation scene pivots its PC port from PlayStation to Xbox

Culture Desk★ 225 pts · 236 cmts

Days after Grand Theft Auto VI went on sale, the poster known as Cyberleek put up what the thread describes as a full Xbox build executable of the game.[11] The leak points inward, one reader argued: Sony had pushed multiple PlayStation 5 firmware updates in the weeks before release, likely to prevent the game being dumped — but “this is a leak from within the company or someone who seemingly has had access for some time, considering they have a full Xbox build.”[12]

For the recompilation scene, the leak redraws the plan: efforts had been betting on a vulnerable PS5 firmware dump and tools like Kyty to translate the PlayStation version to PC; with an Xbox build in hand, public work has shifted to recompiling Xbox shaders and calls into standard PC ones. The main effort — Leonida, on GitHub, run in public with tasks claimed through issues — was, per its Discord, “already close to booting the game just a few hours” after the leak landed; one reader counted five to six pull requests a minute and more than a thousand people coordinating.[12]

Not everyone took the file at face value: one reader could not find it where claimed — “just the option to purchase for $40,” already there on Friday — and asked whether the claim was even true. The thread’s longer question is whether leaks like this speed a future of games streamed from the vendor’s cloud, though the same reader noted that “not even that would prevent it” if the source is inside the house.[12]

Security Briefing · IP

Arm’s lawyers order the takedown of a community AArch64 reference site; it closes Dec. 4

Security Briefing★ 625 pts · 123 cmts

The banner now atop Jon’s Arm Reference, at arm.jonpalmisc.com, reads in full: “PER ARM’S REQUEST, THIS SITE WILL BE DISCONTINUED ON 4 DECEMBER 2026. Arm’s lawyers have notified me to take down this site or face legal action.” The site is a searchable reference for the AArch64 instruction set and the system registers defined by Armv8-A and Armv9-A — “the ISA documentation, but with search and without all the popups and bloat,” as Jon, its author, put it on the thread.[13]

“They are requesting removal stating that I am using their trademarks and IP by hosting their documentation,” he wrote, “which seems hard to work around while still keeping the site useful.” Readers have offered to host mirrors, or suggested he release the code that generates the site — minus Arm’s IP — so anyone can rebuild it; he is looking into all of it, and into ways to keep old bookmarks and links working. To the site’s fans “over the years (even the ones at Arm): thank you for your support.”[14]

The thread’s readings ran from history to strategy. One reader recalled Intel mailing out whole suites of books documenting its architecture — “It seems like chip companies would want folks to know how to use their products” — and another marked Arm as “increasingly closed off” with its internals these past five years, wondering aloud whether the point is “to stave off RISC-V people.” The practical question beneath the eulogy: what is the best way to archive a site like this locally before December 4?[14]

Security Briefing · Hardware

One command turns a MacBook’s trackpad haptic motor into a voice — no speaker anywhere

Security Briefing★ 245 pts · 149 cmts

The trackpad of a MacBook contains no speaker — just a small haptic motor that taps the glass so a click feels pressed. nospeaker, an open-source program from the a90.com collective, drives that motor up to 4,000 times a second, “fast enough to make sound you can hear,” and turns the glass into a voice: it talks, sings, raps, and becomes an instrument under a finger. The show runs about a minute and a half, from one line — curl -fsSL a90.com/nospeaker | sh — and needs a Force Touch trackpad (MacBooks since 2015, or a Magic Trackpad 2 or newer) and macOS 12 or later on Apple Silicon. It was tested on a MacBook Pro with an M5 Max running macOS 27.2.1.[15]

The engineering is a tour of what Apple does not expose. The public haptics API offers three fixed taps; the private MultitouchSupport framework will play any list of sine tones. Every 10 milliseconds, linear prediction finds the voice’s two strongest resonances — its formants — plus loudness and pitch, and the motor plays those two tones, restarted on each beat of the pitch, “so they buzz like a voice instead of whistling.” Measured with the MacBook’s own microphone, the glass is loud from about 100 Hz to 4 kHz and cleanest around 1 kHz. It sounds robotic, the README admits, and it is “much easier to follow when you can read along.”[15]

The thread went straight to the threat model: “Can’t every speaker also be used as a microphone? If so, isn’t this a huge security hole? Either way, what is the best way to turn this functionality off?” Another reader asked whether a motor built for short taps would survive a few weeks of concerts. And one rated the trick beneath the trick more impressive still: “the fact that clicking that unmoving slab feels like pressing a button is far more impressive.”[16]

Practical Tech Corner · Mail

Kagi opens a public beta of Kagi Mail: a @kagimail.com address, 50 GB, and a promise of no ads and no lock-in

Practical Tech Corner★ 271 pts · 209 cmts

Kagi announced the public beta of Kagi Mail — a private email and calendar service with a @kagimail.com address or your own domains — free for 60 days, $99 a year after, and included with a Kagi Ultimate plan. The small team built the full stack: hosting for addresses and domains plus mail and calendar apps, on open standards and open-source infrastructure — the Stalwart server, IMAP, JMAP, and CalDAV — so “you can use Kagi’s apps or any compatible client you already like.” The basics include 50 GB of storage, unlimited aliases, unlimited domains, and a built-in calendar.[17]

The privacy defaults are the pitch: tracking pixels and remote images are blocked or proxied “so senders can’t tell when or where you opened your mail”; new senders are held until you approve them; suspicious senders raise a phishing warning; there are no ads, and AI features carry a one-switch off and draw from an existing allowance. The no-lock-in promise is explicit — one-click export, six months of read access after you leave — and native mobile apps are not due until the third quarter of 2027; until then, Apple Mail, Thunderbird, or any IMAP/JMAP client.[17]

The thread argued price and trust. $99 a year was “far too much” for one reader paying Migadu $19, and another pays Fastmail $60; a third would not trust unencrypted storage at all. A fourth walked away over the billing path: “I was the biggest Kagi fan until I found out that 2% of my monthly fee goes directly to Yandex/Russia.”[18]

Field Desk · Retail

Nespresso’s “Project Olympus” cuts the grams of coffee per capsule, and the internal script tells staff what to say about it

Field Desk★ 237 pts · 265 cmts

Nespresso has been reducing the grams of coffee in its capsules under an internal effort the documents call “Project Olympus” — so reports Alvin Bunk, a coffee blogger who says he was told of the project by a Nespresso employee in August and published the internal text on Nov. 1. The documents say that beginning in 2026, “select Nespresso Vertuo coffee recipes will undergo a recipe optimization” — slightly less coffee per capsule, rolled out gradually from the first through third quarters — while promising the same taste profile, the same coffee volume per cup, and the same caffeine levels, “with only minor natural variation.”[19]

The internal script is the story inside the story: if customers notice that certain capsules feel lighter, staff are told to emphasize “that the update reflects process innovation, not a reduction in value.” Bunk lists Wave 1 as six pods in the first quarter (Double Espresso Scuro, Chiaro, and Dolce; Fortado; Orafio; Ristretto Intenso) and Wave 2 as eight more, date to be announced. By his tracking spreadsheet, “almost all the range of coffees” is affected; readers on his blog report watered-down taste, and Nespresso boutiques, he writes, are allegedly telling customers it was a barcode issue in manufacturing.[19]

The thread tested the claim the way the internal text invites: one reader allowed that roast level, grind size, pressure, time, and volume could plausibly extract more dissolved solids from the same grams — “there are a lot of variables to play with” at Nestlé scale — but added the hard part: “Harder to imagine: that it will taste good.” Another asked the simple question the documents never answer: has anyone actually compared the before and after?[20]

Corrections & Clarifications

FSNotes, removed “without a clear explanation.” The GitHub headline says Apple gave no explanation and no way to resolve the issue. The thread found the explanation: a cached Netherlands App Store listing reads “Oleksandr Hlushchenko has not identified himself as a merchant for this app” — EU trader status, which can lapse. The app remains on sale in the U.S. and UK. No reply from Apple, yes; no reason, not quite.

Ladybird, “built from the ground up, not a fork.” The engine is the project’s own, but the alpha’s rendering leans on production libraries with other ancestries — Skia and ANGLE, both born at Google, and SQLite — as readers noted on the thread. “From the ground up” describes the engine’s origin, not every component in the browser around it.

Nespresso’s “same caffeine levels.” The internal Olympus text promises unchanged taste, volume, and caffeine, “with only minor natural variation,” validated “from a sensory perspective.” The blog’s own comment thread reports watered-down coffee and lower caffeine, and its spreadsheet tracks gram cuts across “almost all the range.” The guarantee is the company’s; the gram counts on the spreadsheet are measurements.

BepiColombo, “reaches Mercury.” The Guardian’s headline, dated Thursday, says the spacecraft reaches Mercury; the article says controllers will fire the thrusters Saturday to send it into orbit, and that science observations do not begin until April. The arrival was scheduled for the weekend; the science starts in the spring.
