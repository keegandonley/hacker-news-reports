One in Three Amazon Black Friday “Deals” Offered No Savings, Audit Finds

Groundwork Collaborative checked more than 350 products from Amazon’s own deal pages for 2024 and 2025; nearly one in ten “deals” cost more than the pre-sale price.

By the Commerce Desk★ 744 pts · 460 comments

WASHINGTON — Nearly one in three products that Amazon promoted as Black Friday and Cyber Monday “deals” sold for the same price or less in the month before the sale, and nearly one in ten cost more than the pre-sale price, according to an analysis released Monday by the Groundwork Collaborative, which examined the prices of more than 350 products on Amazon’s own deal pages for the 2024 and 2025 sale events.[1] The report, “Deal or Decoy? Amazon’s Deceptive Discounts,” is by Breyon Williams, Elizabeth Pancotti and Lindsay Owens.

The audit names its examples. On Black Friday 2025, a pair of yellow Hunter women’s short rain boots “on sale” for $126.99 had cost $79.97 nine days before the event began — 59 percent more for anyone who waited for the deal. On Cyber Monday 2025, men’s UGG Butte boots “on sale” at $179.98 had been $98.40 roughly four weeks earlier, an 83 percent premium.[1] Amazon’s 2025 deals page tells shoppers the company has “handpicked some of the best” discounts available.[1]

“Shoppers who wait for these major sale events may be playing rip-off roulette,” said Owens, the group’s president and co-author.[1] The thread — the day’s most-discussed at 460 comments — split between readers who called the practice false advertising and those who noted it long predates Amazon; the working defense offered most often was a price-history tracker, though one reader replied that tracker prices and checkout prices have lately stopped matching.[2]

MIT’s Make-Anything Course Page Clears 800 Points

The Making Desk★ 808 pts · ▲ +341 since the morning edition

CAMBRIDGE, MASS. — The syllabus page for MIT’s “How To Make (almost) Anything” — the Center for Bits and Atoms course MAS.863, taught Wednesdays from 1 to 4 in building E14-633 — added 341 points since the morning edition, the day’s fastest riser, now the top-scoring link on the front page.[3][4]

The semester runs one fabrication discipline per week: computer-aided design, computer-controlled cutting, embedded programming, 3-D scanning and printing, electronics design and production, machining, input and output devices, molding and casting, and networking, ending with final project presentations on December 15; recitations cover version control, parametric design and debugging.[3]

The thread’s sharpest objection — that CAD alone is a career, so one lecture per tool can teach little — drew the most-repeated reply on the page: “Making things poorly is a lot more interesting than not making them at all. And then you know who to ask when you need part of it done well.”[4]

Systems Desk

Five Banks and Zelle Report Outages at Once

Systems Desk★ 579 pts · 443 cmts

Bank of America, Chase, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo and the payment network Zelle all reported outages on Monday, in the day’s second-most-discussed thread.[5] Readers catalogued the failure modes — cards declined at terminals, physical branches closed or unresponsive, online banking down.[6] The Associated Press report sat behind an automated bot wall at press time, so this account is drawn from the thread and its title.

Two observations organized the 443 comments. First, that simultaneous multi-bank failure is a different order of event from the now-routine single-bank outage. Second, that the closest precedent is Revolut’s late-October outage in Europe, which the company traced to a Google Cloud failure and resolved in about 12 hours — putting the sector’s shared cloud dependencies at the center of the question.[6]

Software Desk

Essay Argues Software Failure Is Now Systemic

Software Desk★ 282 pts · 172 cmts

An essay by developer Jimmy Breck-McKye argues that this month’s failures share no single cause but a common shape: a two-day global Microsoft outage, an FAA ground stop that halted all United States flights, the PHP2Shell vulnerability in a language powering an estimated 70 percent of the world’s websites, and the eMudhra certificate compromise each became cascading failures that complex systems could not recover from.[7]

Artificial intelligence is “both a cause and a symptom,” he writes — attackers can now find complicated exploits in hours, and companies do not divulge whether broken systems were built with AI.[7] The thread disputed particulars: the FAA ground stop followed a cable swap, one reader noted, and npm was hardly the first package manager. The most-upvoted worry likened software’s compounding interdependence to Kessler syndrome in orbit.[8]

Hardware Desk

The Power-Button Ritual That Revives Dead ThinkPads

Hardware Desk★ 278 pts · 110 cmts

A 2007 post by Mike Masnick, resurfaced Monday, records the procedure an IBM support technician taught him for a completely dead ThinkPad: unplug the AC adapter, remove the battery, press the power button ten times at one-second intervals, then press and hold it for thirty seconds. Reinstall the battery, press once — “and she lives.”[9]

The technician called the fault “static discharge”; the thread’s repair hands put the mechanism more exactly as draining residual charge from the capacitors, which is why variants of the ritual work on Dells, MSIs and Toshibas, including laptops with no removable battery.[10] One reader reports a washing machine that reboots the same way: unplug for thirty seconds, open and close the door ten times.[10]

Tools Desk

Jiff 1.0 Puts a Datetime Library Into Rust’s Foundations

Tools Desk★ 250 pts · 54 cmts

Jiff, a datetime library for Rust that places time-zone and daylight-saving handling at the center of its API, reached version 1.0 on Sunday, with author Andrew Gallant pledging to hold the major version “indefinitely” — a deliberate signal that the library is meant to appear in the public APIs of other libraries, where incompatible releases would hurt the ecosystem.[11]

It already ships inside uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu and uutils/coreutils, and underlies a Temporal implementation in AWS’s LLRT runtime.[11] The thread’s single controversy was orthographic: the announcement specifies Jiff is pronounced like “gem,” and a hard-g faction registered its dissent.[12]

TRMNL Announces Frame, a 31.5-Inch Color ePaper Display

The Workshop Desk★ 221 pts · 135 cmts

TRMNL’s third hardware release is Frame: a 31.5-inch full-color ePaper display enclosed in a handmade wooden body, built in a woodworking shop the company opened a few miles from headquarters. The first batch is about 470 units, with a few shipping by the end of 2026 and most deliveries in early 2027.[13]

Frame adds clock-synchronized updates at fixed times of day instead of a fixed refresh interval — less flicker, longer battery life — plus a microSD offline mode, a rechargeable battery of 10,000 to 20,000 mAh, and an optional always-on USB-C port through the back panel.[13] The thread priced the neighborhood: Samsung’s same-panel EM32DX lists around $1,500, a similarly sized InkPoster around $1,700, and one reader put a 4K monitor plus a handmade frame at roughly a third of the cost.[14]

Big-Tech Desk

Wired: Nvidia’s Competition Grew In While It Dominated

Big-Tech Desk★ 211 pts · 205 cmts

The pressures around Nvidia predate Jensen Huang’s exit, Wired reports: the company still holds about 80 percent of AI-accelerator sales, but AMD’s share has climbed from 2 percent in 2022 to between 7 and 10 percent in 2026, and Google, Amazon and Microsoft each design their own silicon to cut costs and dependence.[15] CFO Colette Kress holds the reins while revenue runs from $26.91 billion in January 2022 to $215.94 billion in January 2026.[15]

China supplies the harder margin: barred from selling its most powerful chips there, Nvidia has watched China fall from 20 percent of revenue in 2024 to 8 percent, while Chinese AI company Z.ai says it trained its open-weight models entirely on Huawei chips.[15] The thread doubted the framing — “post hoc rationalization; nothing in here was unknown on 10/29,” one reader wrote, and another noted the forward P/E had already traded below the market average.[16]

Science Desk

Coffeehouses Drove London’s Science, Economists Find

Science Desk★ 171 pts · 98 cmts

A working paper by Monir Bounadi and Jens Oehlen, dated October 31, tests the historians’ claim that coffeehouses fueled the Scientific Revolution. Using newly geocoded records and a staggered event study across seventeenth-century London parishes, it finds that a parish’s first coffeehouse is followed by a persistent rise in applied scientists and scientific-instrument shops, concentrated among scientists with weak family ties to Oxford and Cambridge.[17]

Across European cities of similar size and growth, applied-mathematics titles and instrument makers similarly rose after a city’s first coffeehouse. Calibrated to those estimates, coffeehouses account for 31 percent of London’s applied scientists in the half-century after the first one opened in 1652.[17] The thread reached for modern analogues — makerspaces, forums, this page among them — with the caveat that online spaces are easy to enter and hard to stumble into productively.[18]

Field Desk

Grazing Sheep Raise Solar-Field Bees by 38 Percent

Field Desk★ 166 pts · 135 cmts

DRYDEN, N.Y. — Grazing sheep at two per hectare around solar arrays raised beneficial-insect abundance 38 percent versus mowed control plots, driven by an 83 percent increase in honey bees, Cornell researchers report in a paper published October 7 in Environmental Research Letters, based on three years of trials with the university’s teaching sheep at the Cascadilla Community Solar Farm.[19]

Stocking rate is the dial: at four sheep per hectare, insect abundance matched mowing while vegetation control improved; at eight, vegetation management was maximized and flock health held, but honey bees dropped 45 percent.[19] A companion paper describes a decision-support tool to help farmers grow solar-grazing herds profitably.[19] The thread’s agronomists added a caution about the control: flail mowing is far harder on invertebrates than a power scythe, so the mower matters too.[20]
