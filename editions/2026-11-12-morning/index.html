Nvidia says Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive, effective immediately

CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO and Harvey C. Jones the board's first-ever chair, five days before quarterly earnings

From the AI Desk★ 1153 pts · 1541 comments

SANTA CLARA, CALIFORNIA — Nvidia's board announced on 12 November that Jensen Huang has resigned as president, chief executive officer and member of the board, effective immediately, ending a 33-year run as the company's only CEO.[1] The announcement followed a Wall Street Journal report that the board was preparing to oust him; the Journal's article is behind a paywall, so this paper cites it by headline only.[2] Colette Kress, executive vice president and chief financial officer since September 2013, takes over as interim CEO while keeping the CFO title; the board has opened a search for a permanent successor, considering internal and external candidates.[1]

The board also named Harvey C. Jones — a former CEO and executive chairman of Synopsys — as Nvidia's first-ever chair of the board. "The Board has full confidence in Colette and the leadership team, and we expect the company's operations, customer commitments and product roadmap to continue without interruption," Jones said in the release; Kress committed to "building on the extraordinary foundation Jensen created over the last 33 years." Third-quarter fiscal 2027 results are still scheduled for 17 November at 1:20 p.m. PT.[1]

The thread — the day's most-discussed at 1,541 comments — tracked the fallout: NVDA fell almost 8 percent in after-hours trading, per one reader's live quote, while AMD rose; others noted the five-day gap to earnings and the difficulty of replacing a founder who has run the company since 1993.[3] The timing, several readers observed, follows press reporting on a second affair and earlier reporting that tied Huang to export-control questions — allegations the thread picked over without new facts.[3]

The Tim Peters memorial is now the front page's top score

Language Desk★ 1300 pts · ▲ +647 since the last edition

The memorial thread for Tim Peters, which this paper covered in yesterday afternoon's edition at 653 points, has since more than doubled — 1,300 points at snapshot, the highest score of the day. Guido van Rossum's announcement on the Python Discourse, posted 11 November, remains the underlying news: Peters, author of the Zen of Python and Timsort, died on 15 July.[4][5]

The thread has filled with firsthand recollections. A reader from the comp.lang.python era recalled Peters as "Uncle Timmy," whose careful answers taught a generation to write better code; Gary Robinson, Peters's collaborator on the SpamBayes spam filter, noted that an AI assistant recently reproduced a piece of Peters's 2000s-era Bayesian classification code essentially verbatim — ideas that predate current language models by two decades.[5] Others rounded out the obituary: much of Peters's core-CPython work beyond Timsort concerned large-integer and Decimal arithmetic.[5]

The thread also re-litigated his temporary suspension from Python's community spaces, an episode Peters himself wrote about at length before returning to work on the language — a dispute several mourners asked be set aside for the day.[5]

Field Desk

United Airlines bans AI agents from booking flights for travelers

Field Desk★ 594 pts · 617 cmts

United said on Wednesday it is barring AI agents from making flight reservations on behalf of travelers, citing security risks and the possibility that agent-made bookings could violate federal rules. The airline's statement: "sharing account credentials with personal, third-party, AI assistants can expose sensitive information, and actions taken by these tools may result in unintended purchases, account changes, or use of MileagePlus miles."[8]

United also argued that agent-assisted check-in could breach regulations requiring travelers to verify in person that their luggage contains no banned substances, and said it will restrict agents "directly within our digital platforms and indirectly via travel agencies and aggregators."[8] Analyst Henry Harteveldt read the move as control of the booking funnel: flight searches cost airlines money, and United "doesn't want to add yet another — and potentially even more powerful — intermediary" than search engines and online agencies. Fares stay on Kayak, Hopper and Google Flights; Southwest, by contrast, has made its inventory viewable to ChatGPT users.[8]

The thread priced the objections: one reader put a search at about a cent once global-distribution-system "look-to-book" limits bite, another at a few cents per Sabre call — against thin margins and lost checkout upsells. On enforcement, several readers doubted an airline site can tell a computer-use agent from a human; one e-signature vendor reported going the opposite way, shipping an MCP server so "an agent is a customer."[9]

Follow-Up · Cloud Desk

AWS Emulator gains 419 points — and an answer: it's a maintained product

Cloud Desk★ 899 pts · ▲ +419 since the last edition

Since yesterday afternoon's edition, Amazon's agent-written local emulator of AWS APIs has climbed from 480 to 899 points, and the thread's central question — publicity exercise or real product? — was answered inside the thread itself. An AWS engineer posted that "it's a real product that we plan to maintain," and readers located official documentation at docs.aws.amazon.com/emulator/.[6][7]

The scale of the agent-written codebase became the thread's unit of measurement: roughly 29,000–30,000 commits — 29,684 by one reader's count, enough that GitHub's code-frequency chart reports "too many commits to generate this graph" — against about 6,500 unsorted issues and 12,700 entries in the repository's "things that now-work" ledger.[7]

Practical caveats emerged too: as of v1.0.322 the emulator exposes no CORS headers, defeating browser-based consoles; the MiniStack project's maintainers said they will keep operating until the emulator matches multi-account, multi-region and IAM coverage; and one reader warned that emulated services skip much of AWS's undocumented business logic, so validation errors that production throws will never surface locally.[7]

Culture Desk

A 2019 SIGBOVIK paper measures how many paint splatters parse as Perl: 93%

Culture Desk★ 496 pts · 64 cmts

Colin McMillen's paper for SIGBOVIK 2019 — the Carnegie Mellon satirical-computing conference held each 1 April — answered what it calls "a long-standing open problem in the programming languages community: is it possible to smear paint on the wall without creating valid Perl?" Using OCR on a dataset of 100 paint-splatter images, it found that merely 93 percent parsed as valid Perl programs, and presents seven splatters that do not. It won an "Unwitting Participation Ribbon."[10]

The mechanism is Perl's bareword "unquoted strings": an OCR transcription of one splatter reads lerzfijglpFiji-j, which evaluates to the number 0; another reads ;E,'__',, which evaluates to the string E__.[10]

The thread, on the paper's fourth front-page run since August 2019, supplied the annotations: the splatters are not Perl-specific — one failure transcribes to an expression that also evaluates to −3 in Python — and a footnote reveals a Perl error quine: write Illegal division by zero at /tmp/quine.pl line 1. to the right file and it errors into existence.[11]

Security Desk

Race condition in Apache's HTTP/2 module prompts upgrade to 2.4.71

Security Desk★ 280 pts · 136 cmts

CVE-2026-119618 is a concurrent-execution race in the assign_headers() function of Apache HTTP Server's HTTP/2 module, mod_http2: improper synchronization over shared resources lets a remote, unauthenticated attacker corrupt heap memory, which Wiz — which disclosed details — says can be leveraged into code execution with the web server's privileges. Versions 2.4.55 through 2.4.70 are affected; 2.4.71 patches it, and restricting protocols to http/1.1 is the interim mitigation. Wiz puts vulnerable resources in 64 percent of cloud environments it measures, with 51 percent running publicly exposed instances.[16]

Wiz's own text concedes no exploit was available and no exploitation had been reported at publication; the thread countered with Red Hat's advisory, which rates the flaw 9.8 and states that public research has demonstrated a reliable exploit against a default-configured server. The flaw was found independently by Anthropic and Google Cloud's vulnerability research team.[17]

One reader reproduced an HTTP/2 race against a vulnerable build in seven minutes and about $0.02 of model spend, and verified the patched version did not fail the same way. Others flagged copy errors in Wiz's post, which at one point names the wrong CVE.[17]

GitHub Actions gains first-party workflow lockfiles

Security Desk★ 293 pts · 59 cmts

GitHub Actions now supports workflow lockfiles, and Hugo van Kemenade's walkthrough shows the mechanics: install the gh-actions-lock extension for the GitHub CLI, run gh actions-lock, and the tool rewrites full-SHA action pins back to readable tags while emitting a machine-generated actions.lock file that fixes the exact commits behind them.[14]

The point, readers explained, is transitive dependencies: pinning action X to a SHA never guaranteed that X itself pinned action Y, so a moved tag on a dependency-of-a-dependency still reached your runner. The lockfile pins direct and indirect action calls together.[15]

The caveats came from the maintainer of the auditing tool zizmor: lockfiles currently cover only normal action calls — reusable workflows still need manual hash-pinning, composite actions are locked only transitively — and runs-on: ubuntu-latest remains a mutable runner image nobody can pin by digest.[15]

Jane Street open-sources a Socratic-tutor skill for agents

Practical Tech Corner★ 412 pts · 96 cmts

Jane Street has published socratic-tutor, a 102-line agent skill in its open agent_skills repository: when a user asks to be taught, quizzed or "drilled to mastery," the agent runs a 3–5 question diagnostic, breaks the topic into 4–10 subskills tracked at levels 0–5, and then teaches "primarily by asking questions that force the user to reason, predict, and explain — not by lecturing," with spaced review and a final assessment.[12] A guard clause keeps it out of one-off "explain X" questions.[12]

The thread's verdict was mixed. A user of a week's standing said it works — "it stops the model from the usual handholding" — while allowing "most of the effect can be replicated with this line: 'When the user is wrong, do not give the answer.'" Others noted a GitHub search returns 92 repositories for "socratic tutor skill," and dismissed skills generally as "just a prompt."[13]

Culture Desk · Essay

An Emacs user asks what the editor is for when agents write the code

Culture Desk★ 267 pts · 122 cmts

A long essay posted as a plain-text file recounts a year of Claude Code from the perspective of a decades-long Emacs user: the magit keychords he polished — C-x g, stage, commit — reduced to a grayed-out "commit" prompt answered with tab-return, and every advantage he claimed for the editor, from speed to the expressiveness of Lisp, "vanished the moment we stopped typing and started telling the AI what we wanted."[18]

The thread took both exits. One reader now runs Claude Code inside Emacs and predicts "a new golden era," since the editor's benefits arrive "without having to learn elisp"; others contested the essay's claim that a full terminal emulator was replicated "over a coffee break," and argued the freedom on offer — software that does whatever you imagine, that no one understands — inverts rather than extends Stallman's.[19]

The Science Desk
AI · Machine Learning Research

Apple trains small networks to speed up vector retrieval for RAG

Science Desk★ 221 pts · 38 cmts

Apple Machine Learning Research presents work from ICML 2026, "Amortizing Maximum Inner Product Search with Learned Support Functions": instead of clustering keys and routing queries to the nearest centroid — the standard approximate-search shortcut, which can miss a nearest neighbor sitting far from its cluster's center — Apple trains small neural networks offline, once per database, to approximate the set's support function, whose gradient returns the best-matching key.[20]

Two architectures split the work: SupportNet, an input-convex network that learns the support function per cluster for exact routing, and KeyNet, which directly outputs the best-matching key vector, kept geometrically grounded by a consistency loss from Euler's theorem.[20] The thread was skeptical of retrieval generally — "RAG can take the intelligence of an LLM and dumb it down to old school Google search results," one reader wrote — with several reporting that a plain BM25 index plus an LLM-driven search tool beats learned methods at small scale.[21]

Corrections & Clarifications

RCE, demonstrated? Wiz's headline calls CVE-2026-119618 a "critical RCE"; its own text says no exploit was available and none had been seen in the wild. The code-execution claim comes from the exploitability of the heap corruption — which Red Hat, per the thread, rates 9.8 — not from a published exploit.

93 percent of what? The claim is not that splatters are programs but that OCR transcriptions of them parse: 93 of 100 transcribed images produced valid Perl, chiefly via bareword strings. One "failure" also evaluates to −3 in Python, so even the failures are language-dependent.

Ouster or resignation. The Journal's headline: Nvidia "is preparing to oust" Huang. Nvidia's release, hours later: he "has resigned." The release's verbs leave the board's role unstated; readers can weigh the appointment of a first-ever chair accordingly.

"United bans AI agents." The policy targets "unauthorized, third-party personal AI agents," applied through United's platforms, agencies and aggregators. Bookings through Kayak, Hopper and Google Flights are unaffected, and United does not describe how it will detect an agent.
