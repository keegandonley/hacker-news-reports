Carnegie Mellon Posts Full Semesters of Computer Science Lectures on YouTube, Free

CS Learn opens with thirteen courses from seven departments — one premiering each day this week, five more in January — and the professor who runs it answered the launch thread within hours.

PITTSBURGH — By the Classroom Desk★ 1820 pts · 245 comments

Carnegie Mellon’s Kenneth C. Griffin School of Computer Science has launched CS Learn, a free YouTube channel carrying full semesters of lectures from current courses. The November catalog lists eight courses — from 10-605 Machine Learning with Large Datasets, taught by William Cohen, to 15-442 Machine Learning Systems, taught by Tianqi Chen and Zhihao Jia — with two premiering today, two more on Wednesday and two on Thursday; five further courses, including Mor Harchol-Balter’s 15-259 Probability and Computing, arrive in January 2027.[1][2]

The program is run by Phillip Compeau, who teaches in the Griffin School and introduced himself on the Hacker News thread: students built tooling that automates much of the production work, so “a small team can get a course out quickly and still have people review every lecture.” Asked about the obvious precedent, he conceded it outright: “Yes, MIT got here 25 years ago.” Next on the roadmap are self-paced courseware with instant feedback and badges, then AI tutoring built into that courseware.[1][2]

The thread’s sharpest requests concerned independence from YouTube: one reader asked for a PeerTube mirror, and Compeau replied that the program already runs a Panopto server for internal use and will look into it. The story drew 1,820 points by snapshot — the day’s largest by more than a thousand — and 245 comments.[2]

BugMeNot Closes, Leaving Thanks and a Wall of Code

Culture Desk★ 615 pts · 278 cmts

BugMeNot, the site where strangers deposited working logins for sites that demanded registration, has closed. The entire domain is now two sentences — “BugMeNot has closed. Thanks to everyone who envisioned a better internet.” — followed by an unexplained grid of twenty-five rows of five-letter code groups, and nothing else.[3][4]

The thread became an obituary and a legal seminar at once. “If it ever worked at scale, it would be shut down legally, and by its nature, it can’t really work not at scale,” wrote one reader; replies debated whether password-sharing breaches contract law at all, since the site’s operators never signed any agreement with the sites whose logins were shared.[4]

Malware Served From Optimizely’s Own CDN Reached 178 Organisations

Security Desk★ 461 pts · 192 cmts

On November 16, malicious JavaScript was served to visitors of 178 organisations through the Optimizely Web experimentation platform — Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab, IKEA, HSBC, Equifax, Air France-KLM and Ledger among them. The attacker wrote code into each customer’s “Project JavaScript” field, so the files kept serving from cdn.optimizely.com with Optimizely’s own certificate, from a host every affected site already trusted.[7][8]

The payload, rebuilt 26 times during the day, swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses — checksum-validated, across Bitcoin, Ethereum, TRON, Solana and more — in pages, clipboards and outgoing requests; skimmed card numbers from sites’ own forms (Luhn-checked, though not from hosted payment iframes); stole cookies and html2canvas screenshots; and served iOS exploit chains, Coruna for iOS 13–17 and DarkSword for 18.x, to devices on 18.6 or earlier. Attacker wallets received about $7,040 in roughly nine hours; cards and cookies cannot be counted. Cleanup went from 169 infected snippets at 20:56 UTC to one of 188 at 21:52.[7]

How the attacker obtained write access across unrelated customer projects remains unexplained. The thread worked the defense angle: subresource integrity proves the file you expected but “doesn’t ensure that script doesn’t load other scripts … You need CSP for that,” one reader noted — and platforms like Optimizely exist precisely to change their script, which is what made the injection hard to pin.[8]

A Mystery Domain Ranks Eighth on a Web Popularity List

Field Desk★ 377 pts · 155 cmts

A backlink monitor tipped the author of jack.cab to siterate.org, a site that ranks webpages by TLD — and whose .com table reads google.com first, facebook.com second, youtube.com third, and, at position eight, between apple.com and googletagmanager.com, the string w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com. The domain does not resolve — Firefox returns dnsNotFound — yet Majestic’s Site Explorer credits it with 241,085,378 nofollow backlinks across 5,163,708 referring domains, all “fresh,” meaning from the last 120 days. Ahrefs, for comparison, sees 53 backlinks.[11][12]

Certificate Transparency records show the domain was first bought on November 11 — after most of its backlinks. The essay, published November 15, ends unresolved; the domain is simply the fastest-growing entry in the top 100 of the Majestic Million, which ranks domains by the number of subnets linking to them. Readers supplied context in the thread: Majestic and Tranco are ranking services, and “Faviconese” — the author’s word — means he identified the .cab sites outranking him as casinos by their favicons.[11][12]

Systems Desk

Microsoft: July Fix Lurked Until a Load Surge Felled 80 Percent of Gateway Servers

Systems Desk★ 370 pts · 285 cmts

Microsoft’s preliminary post-incident report, published November 15, blames last week’s Exchange Online outage on a defect introduced in a July 2026 update to its Microsoft 365 network gateway servers. The defect caused “lock contention inside an internal Windows process which caused the machine’s resources (CPU & memory) to be exhausted,” worsened on machines with more cores — and the load testing had “tended to focus on weaker hardware with fewer cores.”[13][14]

At 01:07 UTC on November 12, Teams failed over to the primary gateway supporting Exchange Online just as nearly 14 percent of gateway capacity sat out of service. Each server failure rerouted traffic to the next healthy endpoint, which then failed the same way; within two hours, more than 80 percent of servers were unresponsive. A rollback was impossible — the defect was months old — so engineers shipped “an update that returned the code path to the previously known good state,” applied from 07:20 UTC on November 13. A final report is due November 20.[13]

The thread fastened on the test-environment gap: “If low end is 4 core and deployment is 128 cores, this is not acceptable,” wrote one reader, and another noted the history — “It was MUTEX spins in Windows 95, it’s lock contention in 2026.”[14]

Jiff 1.0 Commits Its Datetime API Indefinitely for Rust

Language Desk★ 407 pts · ▲ +157 since the last edition

Andrew Gallant’s Jiff, a datetime library for Rust that puts time zones and daylight-saving time “front and center” in its API, declared 1.0 on November 15 with an unusual pledge: unless a significant defect is found, the API holds at 1.0 indefinitely. The rationale is adoption — Jiff is already used in production by uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils, and as the basis for a Temporal implementation in AWS’s LLRT, which makes semver-incompatible releases an ecosystem-wide cost.[9][10]

The thread read less as news than as a settling of the standard: “Jiff will probably be the default datetime library for a lot of Rustaceans (and LLMs) in the years to come,” wrote one reader, and contributors described the maintainer’s review style firsthand. The story rose from 250 points at the last edition’s snapshot to 407 this morning.[10]

Tools Desk

OpenCode Browse Lets Coding Agents Drive Your Real Browser

Tools Desk★ 304 pts · 185 cmts

OpenCode has released Browse, an MCP server paired with a Chromium extension that lets coding agents use the user’s real browser — with its tabs, logins and extensions. Agent tabs open in the background in collapsed groups, where the agents read pages, click, type, take screenshots and record network traffic; when a login, 2FA prompt or payment is required, the tab is handed back to the human and the agent continues once they click Continue.[15][16]

Supported browsers are Chrome, Edge, Brave, Arc, Helium, Vivaldi, Opera and Chromium — not Firefox, which one reader questioned; a reply explained that “it’s an extension, so the api surface is different on FF.” Any MCP client works, including OpenCode, Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and Zed. The thread also carried pushback on the business model, with one reader arguing the project is “angling for commercial viability status under the guise of being ‘open’” after usage limits and a subscription tier appeared.[16]

Science Desk

Essay Argues Chemistry’s Qualitative Behavior Is Read Off as Geometry

Science Desk★ 266 pts · 120 cmts

A Substack essay makes the case that theoretical biologists should know Martin Feinberg’s chemical reaction network theory: results such as the Deficiency Zero Theorem let qualitative questions — whether a reaction can oscillate, or admit multiple steady states — be answered from the geometry of the reaction network itself, rather than by estimating the hundreds of rate constants a model of, say, the Krebs cycle might carry. “Without theory we are blind to the landscape of what is actually possible,” the author writes.[17][18]

Feinberg’s own framing, quoted in the essay: absent an overarching theory, “we would be forced to study complex chemical systems on a case-by-case basis, and each new case would be fraught with terrible analytical difficulties.” The thread split on the epistemology — “we really ought to stop mistaking the map for the territory,” wrote one reader, drawing replies that experimental results, not mathematical appeal, decide empirical science.[17][18]

Culture Desk

First National Study Finds Strict School Phone Bans Lift Test Scores

Culture Desk★ 225 pts · 116 cmts

School cellphone bans are beginning to modestly improve academic performance, according to the first large national analysis to compare students’ test scores before and after bans, published Tuesday by Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research. Schools with strict bans — requiring phones locked in pouches all school day — saw math and reading improve as if students had an additional week or two of school a year.[19][20]

The study followed 3.3 million students in third through tenth grade at 4,600 U.S. schools from 2021 to 2026, using fall-to-spring MAP test growth to compare strict-ban schools with similar schools that had no ban. The annual gains reversed about half of the average annual loss students recorded before the pandemic. “The earlier studies on cellphone bans have been dismissed as implying small impacts,” said Thomas Kane of the Harvard Graduate School of Education, an author of the study; “But we think that’s mistaken.”[19]

Thread readers, several self-identifying as former students, argued the harm arrived with around-the-clock social media rather than phones per se — sharing MP3s over infrared at recess being, one wrote, “a far cry” from a device that is “one big 24/7 public group.”[20]

Corrections & Clarifications

“Gradually,” precisely. The headline says phone bans are “gradually improving test scores”; the measured effect applies to strict bans — phones locked in pouches all school day — and equals “an additional week or two of school a year,” reversing about half of the average pre-pandemic annual loss. Looser bans are not what the study measured.[19]

“$7,040 stolen” is the countable part only. Report URI’s figure covers on-chain wallet transfers in roughly nine hours. “We cannot see how many cards or cookies reached” the attacker’s server — the skimming and cookie theft are real but unmeasured.[7]

Thirteen courses, not the curriculum. “Carnegie Mellon is putting computer science courses on YouTube” describes a launch catalog — eight courses premiering this week, five in January 2027 — with more promised but unnamed. The operator’s own benchmark for the effort: “MIT got here 25 years ago.”[1][2]

The “monster” had a shape. The Register’s headline frames a monster outage; Microsoft’s report locates it in one component — the Microsoft 365 network gateway — where more than 80 percent of servers went unresponsive, and where Exchange Online, alone among major services, could not fail out “for scale reasons.” Teams, OneDrive and SharePoint failed over.[13]
