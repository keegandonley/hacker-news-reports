Lead Story · AI Desk

GPT-6 Astra edits a small Qwen’s weights directly and lifts its math score sixfold

MIT CSAIL calls the technique Weight-Space Programming: no training loops, no gradients — the model reads what a network computes and rewrites 0.016% of it.

AI Desk☆ 1,308 pts · 412 comments

CAMBRIDGE, MASSACHUSETTS — GPT-6 Astra, reading only the raw numbers inside a neural network, can identify what the network was trained to do — and rewrite those numbers to change it, four researchers at MIT CSAIL report in a paper posted November 2 by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab. With no environment feedback at all, the model wrote small game-playing networks from scratch that outplay networks trained with PPO for 100 million steps. The authors call the technique Weight-Space Programming.[1][2]

The headline demonstration targets Qwen3.5-0.8B. Asked to make the 0.8-billion-parameter model better at mathematics without gradients, Astra located a defect in its weights that causes reasoning loops — a failure Qwen’s own model card warns about — and corrected it by editing 0.016% of the weights. At identical sampling settings, MATH-500 accuracy rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, and the correction generalized out of the box to instruction following, roughly tripling IFEval. The model was not allowed to write a training loop; it reasoned about what each part of the network computes, or should compute, and changed the numbers accordingly.[1]

The thread read the result as a marker on the road to recursive self-improvement. One reader noted that labs already train models for RSI, “so most likely the same training is useful for improving open source models, as long as guard rails are lax enough.” Another admitted that, asked that morning whether an LLM’s native grasp of weights was less, equal to, or greater than a human’s grasp of neurons, he would not have guessed this.[2]

Ubuntu reaches week three without a fix for CVSS 9.8 kernel flaw

Debian patched the TCP bug on October 16; every supported Ubuntu release is still listed “Vulnerable.”

Systems Desk☆ 1,159 pts · 557 cmts

A CVSS 9.8 flaw in the Linux kernel’s TCP code — present since 2016 and reachable remotely — remains unpatched on every supported Ubuntu release, LTS included, more than two weeks after it was rated, setting off the day’s largest discussion. The post at chalmovsky.com reports that Debian shipped its fix on October 16, and that volunteer-run distributions — Debian, Arch, Alpine — patched within days while “a company that sells security subscriptions” has not; the author states his facts as of 14:48 UTC on November 2.[3][4]

According to the post, Anthropic built a working exploit that takes an affected server to root — a claim the post attributes to Anthropic’s disclosure. Ubuntu’s site promises “timely patches for critical vulnerabilities”; the author writes that he has taken his Ubuntu servers offline entirely and is migrating, having “completely lost trust” in the distribution.[3]

The commentariat widened the indictment. One reader noted that Amazon was already shipping patches for Amazon Linux 2 while Ubuntu waited; others described migrations to Debian or AlmaLinux, some dating their unease to the Ubuntu Pro era. A dissenting reader condensed the other side — “the free operating system that I use is not giving me enterprise level security updates” — and suggested the aggrieved fix it upstream.[4]

AI Desk · Business

Anthropic’s internal Claude use, priced at retail, passed its payroll in April

AI Desk☆ 250 pts · 203 cmts

Anthropic’s IPO filing shows the value of Claude used inside the company, calculated at the prices Anthropic charges customers, exceeded its monthly employee compensation — excluding stock awards — in April, Business Insider reports. By September, internal AI usage was up more than 250-fold since January, and more than 90% of the code added to Anthropic’s software that month was written by Claude.[11][12]

The number is a valuation exercise, not a cash comparison: as the article itself states, Anthropic is not actually spending more money on AI than on salaries — it is metering its own consumption at list prices. The author, who proposed in March that tokens be treated as a fourth component of compensation alongside salary, bonus and stock, calls it a watershed anyway.[11]

The thread was less moved. One reader argued any other company would measure internal servers by cost, not retail; another wrote that a dogfooding lab with “a vested interest in proving that AI usage is the future” tells us little, and unmetered users have no reason to economize.[12]

Tools Desk · Reference

Linux syscall tables for 27 architectures, updated with an API

Tools Desk☆ 190 pts · 48 cmts

syscall.sh now gathers every Linux syscall table in one place — numbers, arguments and registers for 27 architectures, spanning kernel versions 3.3 through 7.3. The x86 table lists 441 calls, x86_64 has 369, ARM EABI 426, arm64 326; the maker says an API accompanies the site and a command-line client with an embedded dataset exists from the original release.[15][16]

The thread promptly supplied a correction to file with the reference: on x86_64 and x32, the syscall number travels in eax, not rax, and the upper 32 bits of rax are ignored. A reader learning aarch64 assembly called the tables exactly what was needed.[16]

Language Desk · Names

Names that changed gender in America were mostly moved by girls

Forty-two boy names drifted toward girls; twelve went the other way — and both directions were driven by girls’ parents.

Language Desk☆ 208 pts · 231 cmts

An analysis of American naming data traces 42 boy names that shifted toward girls and 12 that moved toward boys — and finds that whichever way a name moved, the push usually came from girls. As a reader quoting the piece in the thread summarizes: 34 of the 42 names moved because more girls received them, not because fewer boys did, and 9 of the 12 reverse cases moved because fewer girls received them, not because more boys did.[13][14]

The thread’s working theory is that one famous carrier flips a name — several readers credit Game of Thrones for Arya — and its working caveat is false friends: Jean and Jan are men’s names in French and Central Europe, women’s names in English, a distinction the charts cannot draw. The article itself sat behind a browser-verification wall at press time; its counts are quoted as they appear in the discussion.[14]

Smart glasses decode silently mouthed words with a radar sensor

Subvocal, from a YC F26 team of two, converts unspoken words to text at the speed of speech.

Tools Desk☆ 412 pts · 237 cmts

The Subvocal Company — Aarush and Arnav, a two-person YC F26 team — showed glasses that convert silently mouthed words into text “at the speed of speech” using a radio sensor aimed at the wearer’s face; the post links a video of “Dave from YC” trying the prototype. The clarifying detail sits in the founder’s edit: lips need not move and the mouth need not open, because “the RF can see inside your mouth” — the system can be fine-tuned on internal speech, distinct from the involuntary tongue motion of silent reading.[5][6]

Readers probed the form factor — why glasses, and whether a throat microphone would serve — with one reporting from experience that throat mics “don’t work at all,” and others pointing to rough open-source attempts (subvox, lipflow) aimed at libraries, planes and co-working spaces. Several asked for the technical details the landing page does not yet carry; the founder’s answers live, for now, in the thread.[5][6]

Field Desk · Maps

Central Warsaw rebuilt in one-metre voxels from open data

Field Desk☆ 171 pts · 31 cmts

An interactive map rebuilds central Warsaw in one-metre voxels from OpenStreetMap, city LiDAR scans and the city’s tree database. Visitors can walk to the Palace of Culture, set the date, hour and weather — or, in the project’s own verb, “possess a pedestrian.” The maker cites Minecraft, MagicaVoxel and Dungeon Keeper as influences, and a reader pointed to Streets of Prague as a cousin.[17][18]

Scope and speed drew the caveats. The model covers part of Śródmieście; the Old Town and most of the right bank are absent, and a reader familiar with the area reports blocks near Plac Zamkowy rendered with wrong colors and heights — a 20-story tower where five stories stand. Readers on discrete-GPU laptops reported frame times around 120 milliseconds.[18]

Security Desk · Infrastructure

Second wave of attacks runs supermarket freezers warm in seven countries

Security Desk☆ 333 pts · 382 cmts

Supermarket chains in at least seven European countries — Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Finland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden — reported refrigeration failures overnight, with cabinet temperatures pushed as high as 50C, in a second wave of intrusions echoing last month’s attack in France. The Guardian reports the French incident seized cooling units in as many as 700 supermarkets, causing fires and food losses estimated in the tens of millions of euros.[7][8]

The EU’s food and agriculture commissioner, Christophe Hansen, called the repeated attacks “a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain and undermine consumers’ confidence in it.” Sweden’s Coop told Aftonbladet six shops were hit; ICA said “a limited number of stores” were affected, and one ICA shop in Sjöbo posted that its fridges and freezers had been hacked.[7]

The thread’s sharpest question was timing: the French attack was October 12, three weeks before this wave, and readers asked why chains had not updated refrigeration software or taken the units offline in the interval — one citing a Swedish store manager whose security review began only after the second incident.[8]

Ubuntu LTS kernels listed unfixed for the 9.8-rated TCP flaw

Security Desk☆ 159 pts · 27 cmts

A tracker hosted on freenode.net lists Ubuntu’s 22.04, 24.04 and 26.04 LTS kernels as still unfixed for CVE-2026-98432, with no vendor package and no announced ETA — an exposure the page says matters most for internet-facing and multi-tenant hosts that rely on LTS kernels for isolation. The facts are corroborated by the chalmovsky.com post in the lead spread.[19][20][3]

The host itself carries a caution the thread supplied: since a 2021 takeover, one reader writes, freenode.net has been run by parties “actively hostile to open source software,” and the original Freenode database no longer exists. Treat the page as a tracker, not an authority. Red Hat’s mitigation guidance, cited alongside, “reduces exposure … but cannot fully prevent it.”[20]

Field Desk · Archiving

A web archivist who lost a favourite site explains how to back up yours

Field Desk☆ 296 pts · 73 cmts

The post opens with a confession from inside the trade: its author works in web archiving and still lost teapuppies.com — an Ochaken fansite online since 2004 and essentially unchanged since 2005 — to a lapsed domain now serving gambling ads. “I could have archived that site while it was still around, but I didn’t.”[9][10]

The remedy walks through ArchiveWeb.page, a browser extension that captures pages into WARC and WACZ archive files — formats the author notes record complete requests and responses, headers included, enough to replay JavaScript-heavy sites. A finished WACZ can be kept, mailed to a friend or republished with the open-source ReplayWeb.page viewer; “lots of copies keeps stuff safe.”[9]

The thread swelled with parallel practice: wget for static blogs, SingleFile for one-offs, gwern’s archiving guide for the thorough — and one reader who watched buthowdoitknow.com vanish and rebuilt its visual CPU emulator from memory with an LLM’s help.[10]
