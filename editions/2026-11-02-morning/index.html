Lead Story · Culture Desk

HackerTube indexes 6,454 videos recommended in HN comments

Andrej Karpathy used AI to sift 39,834 recommendations from 2007–2026 into a video site with 150 topics and no user accounts.

Culture Desk★ 2,689 pts · 598 comments

UNITED STATES — Andrej Karpathy built HackerTube from 6,454 YouTube videos recommended in 39,834 Hacker News comments dating from 2007 to 2026. His post says an AI workflow filtered HN comments, used Sonnet to identify recommendations and organized the surviving videos into 150 topics; the site serves a YouTube-like interface, but recommendations do not require an account.[1][2]

Karpathy says it took about two hours. The site uses video frames at the midpoint instead of uploaded thumbnails, keeps its recommendation state in the browser session, offers keyboard shortcuts and exports its data. In the thread, he reports 504 million input tokens, about 90% cached, and 7.6 million output tokens, with more than 90% of the usage going to Sonnet labeling calls; app building and deployment used about one million Opus 5.5 tokens.[1][2]

He says his manual steps were creating a Google Cloud API key for video metadata, starting a VPS and buying the domain; he directed the rest through Claude Code. Those are the author’s reported process and usage figures, not an independently measured benchmark.[1][2]

HackerBooks counts 16,500 books mentioned by HN readers

A successor to HackerTube tracks books cited by at least two commenters.

Culture Desk★ 1,610 pts · 265 cmts

HackerBooks collects roughly 16,500 books mentioned by at least two different Hacker News commenters between 2007 and 2026, according to its maker’s Show HN post. Each entry links back to the comments that mentioned it, and the dataset is downloadable from the site’s About page.[3][4]

The maker says Claude wrote almost all of the data pipeline and site under human direction. The site ranks mentions, not approval: a complaint and a recommendation both increase a book’s count. The homepage shuffles its books daily rather than displaying a fixed popularity list.[3][4]

Tools Desk · Release Watch

PostgreSQL 19 slips toward November as bug fixes mount

Robert Haas reports a sharp rise in back-patched commits and three late feature reversals.

Tools Desk★ 617 pts · 376 cmts

PostgreSQL 19 is likely to ship in November, later than the mid-September to mid-October window that has held for major releases over the past decade, committer Robert Haas writes. Three substantial features were reverted between August 27 and September 15: UPDATE/DELETE FOR PORTION OF, SQL Property Graph Queries and ALTER TABLE MERGE PARTITION/SPLIT PARTITION.[5][6]

For April 8 to September 25, Haas counts 724 commits to the current stable branch in 2025 against 1,169 to its 2026 counterpart, an increase of more than 60%. Back-patches to the prior stable branch rose from 248 to 433, more than 74%. He attributes much of the load to bugs found with LLMs: discovery has accelerated more than human review and fixing. He says diverted review time also delayed deciding what to remove from version 19.[5]

A reader in the thread points to verification capacity as the limiting step: reports and draft fixes still have to be checked by maintainers. Haas calls the delay modest and a consequence of the project’s quality standards, not evidence that PostgreSQL 19 is abandoned.[5][6]

Security Desk · Ask HN

TV privacy thread converges on keeping the display offline

A buyer wants a 60–80-inch 4K screen, at least 60 Hz, to use only with Apple TV.

Security Desk★ 509 pts · 622 comments

An Ask HN reader seeking a privacy-conscious 60–80-inch 4K television with at least a 60 Hz refresh rate asked whether a display can be used only as an Apple TV screen. The most repeated practical answer in the thread is to choose a display and never connect the television itself to a network; a separate player then handles streaming.[7][8]

One commenter recommends Jellyfin for a self-hosted media setup. Another warns that a guest could connect the screen to a phone hotspot later. A claim that disconnected televisions already use neighbors’ open Wi-Fi or cellular links drew a direct challenge: other readers asked for documented cases, and none were supplied in that exchange. Offline use is a recommendation, not a guarantee against every possible data path.[8]

AI Desk · Benchmarks

Framework test measures agents across five builds per stack

A Pokémon app benchmark compares build cost, code size and Lighthouse results; its author warns against generalizing to interactive apps.

AI Desk★ 406 pts · 314 comments

Nolan Lawson asked Opus 5.5 to build the same small Pokémon app against a conformance suite in several JavaScript frameworks and compared median results from five builds per stack. He measured wall-clock time, token cost, code size and Lighthouse performance and accessibility. Angular produced only three measurable apps for some metrics after two runs failed to meet the deployment requirement.[9][10]

SvelteKit, Gracile with Lit and Angular were among the more expensive builds in this test; Lawson says the agents repeatedly read unfamiliar framework material in some runs. Astro performed well on build time and shipped no client-side JavaScript for this content-oriented app, according to a commenter. Another reader notes that this advantage may not carry over to a highly interactive application.[9][10]

Lawson explicitly limits the experiment to one relatively low-interactivity app, with stochastic agent behavior and variable Lighthouse readings. His quoted dollar figures are API-equivalent costs; he says his actual use was covered by a subscription.[9]

Culture Desk · Open Source

OccupySaaS proposes clean-room replacements for paid platforms

Its first two projects target identity and CRM; both are early and their compatibility is limited.

Culture Desk★ 381 pts · 228 comments

OccupySaaS is organizing AI-assisted, Apache-2.0, self-hostable replacements for subscription software. Its public site names two early projects: ZeroAuthy, an Auth0-compatible identity server, and SalesCompel, a Salesforce-oriented CRM with a Go/PostgreSQL core and a Vue editor.[11][12]

The group says its process separates public-spec writers from implementers, records provenance in CLEAN_ROOM.md, builds contracts before code and tests implementations against vendors’ public SDKs. ZeroAuthy lists OIDC discovery, JWKS, PKCE and selected SDKs; SalesCompel lists Account and Contact CRUD, object descriptions, limited queries and external-ID upsert. The site itself says neither project is production-ready.[11]

In the HN discussion, readers question whether buying AI capacity from major vendors is an effective response to layoffs and SaaS pricing. The project’s claim is narrower than replacing complete commercial products today: it offers open implementations of specified interfaces with compatibility still under test.[11][12]

Wikihack ranks Wikipedia links shared on HN

The third project in the day’s HN-remix cluster provides a scrollable article feed.

Tools Desk★ 293 pts · 89 comments

Wikihack scrapes Wikipedia links in Hacker News submissions and comments and ranks articles by how often they are linked, its maker says. It presents a scrollable feed with links back to the submissions and comments that cited each article; a Rising view emphasizes the past 12 months.[13][14]

The maker says the Random view is slightly weighted toward frequently linked articles and that the site is served entirely by its frontend. Asked why the desktop layout resembles a mobile feed, the maker says the design keeps article and HN-comment context side by side. These are link-frequency signals, not editorial judgments of accuracy or importance.[13][14]

Tools Desk · Show HN

OmniDiff applies type-specific algorithms to files beyond code

The AGPLv3 tool handles code, images, PDFs, archives and configuration files.

Tools Desk★ 274 pts · 81 comments

OmniDiff is a Rust-based, AGPLv3 diff tool that routes files to content-specific comparison pipelines rather than coloring a standard line diff. Its maker says it handles code in 25 languages as well as configuration, images, PDFs and archives, and can diff C++ files of tens of thousands of lines in under 100 milliseconds on a 2023 ThinkPad T14s.[15][16]

The maker studied 2,000 sampled diffs from 7,400 Gentoo-listed repositories before building the pipelines. The repository calls the current release version 0.3.0 and reports content-specific algorithms for 98.8% of changes, while explicitly noting that PDFs have fewer than ten judged test cases. In the thread, the maker says moved-code comparisons are much better than Delta’s, but that is a developer claim, not a neutral head-to-head measurement.[15][16]

Outcrop overlays geologic maps on a phone camera view

Bedrock data can be saved offline; some live layers still need a connection.

Science Desk★ 228 pts · 48 comments

Outcrop overlays geologic maps on a landscape seen through a phone camera, identifying the mapped rock, its age and how it formed. It uses Macrostrat geologic maps and Christopher Scotese’s PALEOMAP reconstructions to show where a rock’s continent lay when it formed.[17][18]

Users can save an outlined area so the map and camera view work without a signal; fossil finds and active faults in the live camera view still need a connection. The developer says Android is publicly available and the iPhone version is in beta. In the thread, a reader reported Android tracking failing to start, while the developer said a web version would be difficult because the AR view relies heavily on ARCore and ARKit.[17][18]

Science Desk · Citizen Data

Radiaverse maps dose-rate tracks from pocket detectors

Community measurements are public; readers caution that device differences can distort apparent hotspots.

Science Desk★ 192 pts · 53 comments

Radiaverse combines location and dose-rate tracks uploaded by RadiaCode detector owners into a public world map. Its site says a detector logs dose rate and location every second; users can publish tracks anonymously, inspect individual measurements and add contextual areas with photos. An account is required to publish, not to browse.[19][20]

The site reports 9,090 square kilometers of unique street-resolution coverage in the United States and 2,332 in Germany at the fetched page. In the thread, readers caution that zoom levels and apparently inconsistent devices make some hotspots hard to interpret; one commenter says a California track was corroborated as a uranium deposit by a geological survey. These are community measurements, not a calibrated public-health assessment.[19][20]

Corrections & Clarifications

“Books recommended” versus books mentioned. HackerBooks requires two different commenters, but counts criticism and praise alike. Its ranking is a frequency index, not a recommendation score.[4]

“PostgreSQL 19 is late” versus canceled. Haas expects November, perhaps later; his release explanation also names three features reverted late in beta. The release has not been described as canceled.[5]

“Drop-in replacements” versus current scope. OccupySaaS says both first projects are early, SalesCompel supports only limited queries, and the site does not call either production-ready. Compatibility is a goal being tested against public SDKs, not a complete replacement today.[11]

“Radiation hotspots” versus calibrated readings. Radiaverse visualizes community detector tracks. HN readers report apparent device and scale inconsistencies; the map alone does not establish that a location is hazardous.[19][20]
